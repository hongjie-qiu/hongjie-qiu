## 1. Calibration and mispricing by segment (category, time to expiry, price bucket, liquidity tier)

### Takeaway
Between 10 and 90 cents, within about a week of resolution, and in markets with real volume, both venues are calibrated to within roughly 1–2 cents; that is smaller than the cost of trading. The deviations that are measurable in cents sit at the edges: long horizons (more than a week), Kalshi favourites at 80 cents and above (about +4 points at 7 days), the price tails in the final hour, short-horizon weather (favourite bucket overpriced by up to 11 points on Polymarket), thin Polymarket markets (about 2 cents of longshot overpricing below $20,000 of volume), parlays with five or more legs, and the timing of unscheduled geopolitical events. Single-game sports within 48 hours of the game, finance and scheduled central-bank decisions are the best-priced segments.

### Inferences
- **Size of each deviation in cents (my reading across the studies above).** Liquid, 10–90 cents, within 7 days: 1–2 cents (calibration error 1.1–2.5 points). Kalshi favourites at 80 cents and above, 7–30 days: 4–6 points gross, the largest robust-looking number in a broad sample. Polymarket favourites at 90 cents and above: 0.3–0.8 cents per dollar. Polymarket longshots: 0.8 cents on average, 2.1 cents in $1,000–$20,000 markets, zero above $20,000–$50,000. Short-horizon weather favourites on Polymarket: up to 11 points, from one small study. Long horizons (over a month): slopes 1.2–1.7, i.e. 3–11 points at 70 cents, but with few independent events.
- **MY CALCULATION: what a 70-cent price means under each published fit.** Kalshi politics, 2 days to 1 month (Le, slope only): 82.5%. Kalshi sports, 1 week to 1 month: 74.1%. Kalshi broad sample at 7 and 30 days (Plumbline slopes 1.07 and 1.11): 71.2% and 71.9%. Polymarket 7 days before scheduled end (aidaman01, with intercept): 68.9%; with at least $50,000 of volume: 69.7%. Polymarket 30 days (wcsmars, closure anchor, with intercept): 66.6%. So the "75%" rule of thumb is at the upper end; on Polymarket the YES side is, if anything, slightly overpriced once the intercept is included.
- **The two venues do not show the same bias.** Kalshi leans to the classic favourite-longshot direction in every broad sample (slope 1.07–1.15 at 7–30 days). Polymarket's direction depends on the sample: trade-weighted returns show longshot losses (Cardozo), a random sample shows a small bias that disappears above $50,000 of volume (aidaman01), and high-volume markets show the reverse (slope 0.90, driven by sports at 0.66–0.82). Sports is the one category where every Polymarket source agrees that longshots are *not* overpriced. For sports contracts still open a week before close (season-long and futures markets, not single games) the venues point in opposite directions: Kalshi 1.22 (1.04–1.47) and Polymarket 0.82 (0.68–0.96). Neither study tests whether the same events are priced differently, so this is a lead, not a finding.
- **A methodological trap inflates many published slopes.** aidaman01 shows by simulation that measuring the price "h days before the actual close" on efficient markets that can resolve early produces a spurious slope of 2.34, against 0.945 when anchoring on the scheduled end. Le's time-to-resolution, wcsmars's closure-proxy results and several "days before close" tables are exposed to this; nalimmm avoids it by using fixed-date markets only, and finds the bias only in the last hour. I therefore weight the scheduled-anchor and fixed-date results more heavily.
- **Political "underconfidence" is mostly a small-sample story.** Kalshi politics through 2025 is dominated by the 2024 election, where favourites won; with event clustering the interval is very wide. Broad samples that include 2026 give politics slopes of 1.17–1.18 with intervals that touch 1.
- **Weather is the one category where independent sources agree on a short-horizon error in the opposite direction** (prices too extreme near resolution: Kalshi slopes 0.69–0.97 within 48 hours through 2025; Polymarket favourite bucket overpriced in the last 1–12 hours in 2026), while Kalshi's market mean still beats public forecasts. The error is in the confidence of the distribution, not in its centre.

## 2. Long-tail and newly listed markets (low volume, listing time, text-heavy rules; persistence and tradable size)

### Takeaway
Thin markets are where prices are worst on paper, but most of that is quotes on empty books rather than tradable mispricing: below $1,000 of lifetime volume a third of Polymarket prices sit at exactly 50 cents, and on Kalshi only 28% of sampled markets have a usable two-sided quote a week before close. New listings open with wide spreads that narrow mostly within the first hour, and that is also when language models are most competitive against the price; no source measures how many dollars can be traded at those prices. For rule-heavy contracts, disputes are rare (0.31%) and cannot be predicted case by case; the evidence that careful rule reading pays is two case studies from a $200 pilot.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **"Mispriced" and "tradable" separate sharply at about $1,000–$20,000 of lifetime volume on Polymarket.** Below $1,000 the average price error is 17.5 cents but the quote is not a market. Between $1,000 and $20,000 the longshot overpricing is about 2 cents against half-spreads of 1–2.5 cents and frequently one-sided books. Above $20,000–$50,000 the measured bias is zero.
- **Persistence differs by type of error.** Arithmetic gaps last less than one 2-minute snapshot. Opening-spread width decays within the first hour. An interpretive error about a resolution rule persisted for about five days in the one documented case (22–27 April 2026). A language-model edge against the price decays monotonically from open to close.
- **Tradable size is the unmeasured variable.** The only dollar figures are the pilot's per-market profits of $19 and $46 on $11–$15 of capital with a 60% fill rate. Nothing found supports sizes beyond tens to low hundreds of dollars per thin market.
- **Text-heavy rules are a double-edged segment.** They are where a careful reader found edge, and also where settlement risk concentrates; dispute risk can be ranked across groups of markets but not predicted for a single contract, so position sizes must assume an occasional adverse resolution.

### Gaps
- No study tracks the *opening price* of new listings against outcomes, and none reports depth in dollars at the mispriced level for low-volume markets. The full text of the opening-spread paper (first-hour numbers, per-category costs) could not be opened.
- No measurement of mispricing as a function of rule length or rule complexity was found.
- Venue data terms (as read by one practitioner, not checked against the originals): Kalshi's developer agreement limits API use to a member's own trading and forbids storing data for other purposes; Polymarket's terms effective 11 August 2026 forbid scraping and data access by capital-market firms. — [github.com/aidaman01/prediction-markets](https://github.com/aidaman01/prediction-markets)

## 3. Fee survival: does each documented bias exceed fees plus spread under the November 2026 schedules?

### Takeaway
Every published after-cost test of a simple bias rule found nothing reliably positive for a liquidity taker: selling Polymarket longshots made +0.17 cents per bet (t = 1.26) and lost at double costs; 120 price-bin tests across both venues produced three positive intervals and none after multiple-testing correction; backing Kalshi sports favourites lost 2.4 cents per contract. The favourite and longshot premia on both venues accrue to makers, and the cheapest fee tier (coefficient 0.01, or zero for geopolitics) now makes fees a minor cost on Polymarket outside sports, elections, finance, tech and short crypto, so the spread, not the fee, is the binding cost there. On Kalshi the fee alone is 1.75 cents at 50 cents.

- **Segment scorecard for a slower analytical trader (my synthesis of sections 1–5).**
  - *Might still hold edge, all unproven and small in capacity:* (1) Kalshi and Polymarket contracts 2–14 days from close whose outcome turns on a technical reading of the resolution rule or a data-release convention; (2) the first hours to days after listing, in high-uncertainty contracts, where three models scored +0.09 to +0.17 in Brier skill against the price; (3) contested contracts priced 40–60 cents, where a strong model's large disagreements with the market won 81% of the time in one benchmark (68 cases); (4) Kalshi economic-data series, where one benchmark has Claude Opus 4.5 ahead of the price (+0.163) and web search helping all ten models, Kalshi economics shows a slope of 1.19, and search-enabled agents match the professional consensus on macro nowcasts, none of it tested after costs; (5) short-horizon weather favourites on Polymarket; (6) Kalshi favourites at 80 cents and above through resting orders; (7) niche science and health series where a domain model beats a thin market; (8) Polymarket geopolitics deadline ladders (zero fee, prices weak on timing), with the warning that no account group was found to predict timing beyond the price.
  - *Clearly cannot:* bias rules in liquid sports (single-game prices are calibrated at 0–48 hours, the fee rate is 0.05, and backing Kalshi favourites loses 2.4 cents after costs); any statistical rule in a liquid market at 10–90 cents within a week (the 1–2 cent error is below cost); tail harvesting as a taker; the final hours before resolution (all ten models below −0.7 skill; in-play sports is a step function); central-bank decisions the day before (51 of 51 priced correctly) and finance, which has the smallest maker-taker gap; politics and financial contracts on model forecasts alone (all ten models negative in one benchmark; no poll-based forecast was profitable against Kalshi in 2024); short-duration crypto (bots are 55–62% of volume, rate 0.05); Kalshi daily temperature against public forecasts (the market beats them by about 10%); mechanical recalibration; parlays; and mention markets for a model acting alone.
  - *Conflicting evidence, do not rely on either way:* model forecasting in sports. One benchmark has Claude Opus 4.5 at +0.294 skill against Kalshi sports prices (150 markets in total across categories), while the earlier round found sports to be a weak category for models. Separately, Polymarket sports prices carry the lowest skill against the base rate of any category in four studies (0.264 in the Princeton thesis, 0.62 in carol-747, 11% in Plumbline's live-price sample, and 0.255 by my calculation from aidaman01's Brier 0.1725 against 0.2317), which reflects how close to a coin flip those contracts are, not miscalibration.
- **Makers, not takers, collect the documented premia** on both venues (maker-taker splits in Cardozo, Becker, Bürgi et al., and the floor-cent result). A slow analytical trader who rests limit orders pays no fee on either venue but takes adverse-selection and non-fill risk, which no source quantifies for small accounts.

### Gaps
- The official Polymarket fee table could not be opened; the category mapping for elections, finance, tech, crypto and economics comes from a third-party transcription that agrees with the official snippet and with press coverage.
- No published backtest uses the post-20-November schedule on post-change data (there are only days of it).
- Kalshi maker fees on specific series, and the series-level fee multipliers, were not retrieved.
- No source measures adverse selection or fill rates for small resting orders, so the "maker" route cannot be costed.

### Inferences
- **MY CALCULATION on the ForecastBench file.** A Brier Index of 70.4 corresponds to a Brier score of 0.088, 71.9 to 0.079 and 74.3 to 0.066. Of 528 entries, 105 have a market-question point score above the market-price baseline, 11 have a confidence interval whose lower bound is above the baseline's point estimate, and 3 (Cassi-2026-05-10 and two FutureSearch entries) have an interval entirely above the baseline's interval. With 528 entries, about 13 would clear a one-sided 2.5% bar by chance, so this is not evidence that any system reliably beats the market price.
- **The price itself is worth about three index points to a frontier model, and search closes the rest of the gap.** Tool-less 2026 models score about 67.6 on market questions, significantly below the 70.4 baseline; with web search they score 70–72, statistically tied with it. Within the same file, 2024-era models gained 1.0–4.6 points (mean 2.5) on market questions when given the freeze value.
- **The state of "LLM versus market" in late 2026 is a tie with wide error bars, not a win.** One benchmark puts the newest models level with the market (ForecastBench, FABench on 54 questions), one puts them behind (Forecast-Dojo), and one team reports significantly positive simulated gaps for five newest variants with lower bounds between +0.2 and +3.3 (Gu et al.). - **The only live, cost-inclusive positive record is tiny.** $200, 26 days, 129 markets, and a third of the headline return disappears when six trades are removed. By Foresight Arena's own power arithmetic, several hundred resolved bets are needed to detect an edge of the size being claimed.

## 5. LLM-specific evidence in thin or niche markets, and blends of LLM forecast with market price

### Takeaway
No study shows a language model beating prices specifically in low-liquidity markets; the one thesis that split by liquidity found the market ahead at every level, and most benchmarks filter thin markets out. What the evidence does show is conditional competitiveness: models are closest to or ahead of the price early in a market's life, on contested (near 50 cents) contracts and on contracts the crowd "had not decided", and far behind near resolution and on consensus contracts. A conservative blend that keeps 70% weight on the market beat the market alone in one mention-market study by 0.005 Brier points; cost-adjusted returns exist only for the $200 pilot and for simulated or losing benchmarks.

### Cited Findings
- [A·SS] TU Delft thesis, "Performance of Large Language Models in Prediction Markets": Polymarket prices are consistently more accurate than model forecasts "across all evaluated models, model combinations, prompting strategies, market stages, and liquidity levels of markets". — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:6add2e07-a494-442a-8e6a-ac352db40b19)
- [A·FT] TimeSeek (Kalshi, 150 markets, ten models, 15,000 forecasts; markets with at least $5,000 of volume). By market uncertainty: on toss-ups (price within 10 cents of 50) seven of ten models beat the price, led by Claude Opus 4.5 at +0.301 Brier skill, GPT-5.2 +0.217, Kimi-k2.5 +0.179; on consensus markets (price beyond 90 or below 10) every model is at −0.69 to −1.68. When Claude disagreed with the market by more than 20 points (303 cases), it won 80.9% of toss-ups (68 cases), 65.4% of "hard" markets (52 cases) and 19.8% of consensus markets (81 cases). By category: Claude is positive in Sports (+0.294) and Macro (+0.163); all ten models are negative in Politics and Financial markets. Web search helps every model overall (+0.14 to +0.59) but hurts in 6 of 50 model-checkpoint cells. The best two-model ensemble (Claude with Kimi-k2.5) improves pooled skill from −0.068 to −0.041; "no pair surpasses the market overall". — [arXiv 2604.04220](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04220)
- [A·FT] Blend of model and market in a niche category (Kim et al.; 856 Kalshi earnings-call mention markets, 50 companies, 70 events, April–December 2025; GPT-5.1; lead authors at the startup LinqAlpha, one co-author at Kalshi). Brier: market 0.1441; market-conditioned prompting alone 0.1470; mixture with 0.7 weight on the market 0.1392 and 80.3% accuracy. Prompting that treats the price as a prior beat unconditioned prompting (Brier 0.1470 against 0.1674). When the model disagreed with a market price of 50–60%, the model was closer in 17 of 30 cases. No significance test against the market and no trading simulation are reported. — [arXiv 2602.21229](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21229)
- [A·FT] Counter-evidence for the same category: the Prophet Arena team excluded Mentions from live trading because models "perform substantially worse on these questions". — [arXiv 2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06166)
- [A·ABS] Bayesian Linguistic Forecaster (Murphy; v5, 27 September 2026): on 400 ForecastBench question-date instances it is "the only compared method to significantly outperform a crowd+empirical-prior baseline in terms of overall performance". Its public leaderboard entry (Anthral Labs "blf-ensemble-opus45-gpt55") scores 69.8 [68.8, 70.9] on 515 market questions, against 70.4 for the market-price baseline. — [arXiv 2604.18576](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18576); [leaderboard_tournament.csv](https://github.com/forecastingresearch/forecastbench-datasets/blob/main/leaderboards/csv/leaderboard_tournament.csv)
- [A·FT] A pure "calibrated market anchor" entry on ForecastBench scores exactly the market baseline (70.4), and the market column for frontier models is given in section 4. — [leaderboard_tournament.csv](https://github.com/forecastingresearch/forecastbench-datasets/blob/main/leaderboards/csv/leaderboard_tournament.csv)
- [C·FT] Statistical (not language-model) blend on Polymarket (wcsmars; 55,826 market-horizon observations, 25 test months, markets ending by July 2025). Pooled Brier: market 0.07853; isotonic recalibration 0.07774 (1.0% better; event t = 2.42, equal-month t = −0.32); logistic 0.07784; gradient-boosted model with price-path, structure and text features 0.07987 (1.7% *worse*). Trading on model-versus-price disagreement net of 1 cent: −5.70% per trade at a 2-point threshold, +0.50% at 5 points (t = 0.23), +9.44% at 10 points (event t = 2.79, monthly t = 1.02; 6,214 trades). — [github.com/wcsmars/Polymarket-Calibration-and-Favorite-Longshot-Bias](https://github.com/wcsmars/Polymarket-Calibration-and-Favorite-Longshot-Bias)
- [C·FT] A pre-registered, hash-committed experiment that scores eleven models (including a language-model evidence pipeline, a recalibration model and an ensemble) against each venue's own price has collected 729,512 forecasts and 100,764 resolutions since July 2026; as of 22 August 2026 "No skill claim has been made, and none is due yet"; its confirmatory window closes 31 December 2026. — [github.com/Vladosyna/prediction-market-forecast-lab](https://github.com/Vladosyna/prediction-market-forecast-lab)
- [A·SS] Cheong, "Autonomous LLM Agents Trading in Decentralized Finance Markets" (LSE job-market paper, mid-2026): wallets identified as language-model agents make more accurate trading decisions and earn higher returns on investment than human wallets *within the same Polymarket markets*, with relative accuracy improving after a major model upgrade; one search summary gives a 12.1 percentage-point higher chance of ending on the correct side; agent order imbalance is associated with lower price error. — [paper PDF](https://www.bayes.citystgeorges.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0003/1013997/Wan-hu-Cheong-JMP-July-2026.pdf)

- [A·ABS] Human-plus-model pilot on Polymarket questions (Ming, 2 July 2026): most participants deferred to the model or used it to confirm a prior guess; a minority reached "accuracy matching or even exceeding" the market; preliminary. — [arXiv 2607.02467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02467)
- [A·ABS] Crowds of similar models add little: ten fine-tuned agents have pairwise error correlation 0.70, equivalent to about 1.4 independent forecasters; mixing model families cuts the correlation from 0.68 to 0.40. — [arXiv 2606.26583](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26583)
- [A·ABS] Text signals in thin, non-political Polymarket markets (79 markets): a stance shift in the comment section preceded a change of leading outcome in 23 of 27 evaluable markets, but added "no clear improvement" in block-by-block forecasting. — [arXiv 2609.28965](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28965)

### Inferences
- **The condition that matters is uncertainty and market age, not thinness as such.** Positive results cluster where the price is near 50 cents, where the market is young, and where the crowd has not settled; all of them come from markets that passed a volume filter ($5,000 on Kalshi, "high volume and liquidity" on Prophet Arena). Truly thin markets are untested because benchmarks drop them.
- **The practical recipe the evidence supports is selective deference:** keep the market as the prior (70% or more of the weight), act only on large disagreements in contested, young or rule-technical contracts, and never in the final hours. This mirrors the 0.7 weight in the mention-market study, the 0.10 model weight in the statistical blend, the toss-up result in TimeSeek and the exclusion rules of the live pilot.
- **MY CALCULATION: the blend gains are far below trading cost when expressed as an average.** A Brier improvement of 0.005 at prices near 50 cents corresponds to moving the forecast by well under one cent on average, against a Kalshi fee of 1.75 cents plus spread. The blend can only pay if bets are concentrated in the minority of contracts with large disagreement, which is exactly what the disagreement-threshold backtest shows (negative at 2 points, positive only at 10).
- **Cost-adjusted return evidence is thin and mixed:** one positive live pilot ($200), one negative real-money benchmark ($60,000 across six models), and simulated results in both directions.

### Gaps
- No test of language models on markets below about $5,000 of volume, on newly listed markets in their first hour, or on any category other than mentions with a blended forecast and costs.
- The TU Delft thesis's liquidity breakdown and Cheong's sample sizes, identification of agent wallets and return figures were seen only as search summaries.
- The pre-registered forecast lab has not reported; its result (due after 31 December 2026) would be the cleanest independent evidence on this question.

## 6. AI-agent actors: funds, startups, crypto agent projects, AI-lab and platform tie-ups, and auditable track records

### Takeaway
No fund, startup or agent project has an audited record of profitable language-model-driven trading on Polymarket or Kalshi. The verifiable records are forecasting scores on third-party leaderboards (ForecastBench, Metaculus), one academic real-money benchmark in which all six models lost on Kalshi, one $200 academic pilot, and self-reported aggregate statistics for on-chain agents. The clearest aggregate fact is that automated accounts as a group take money from human-paced retail accounts, but that group is dominated by market makers and arbitrage bots, not forecasters.

### Inferences
- **Ranking by verifiability.** Strongest: public leaderboard scores (forecasting only), the Prediction Arena paper (real capital, losses) and the Gu et al. pilot (real capital, $200, trade log linked from the paper). Middle: Cheong's wallet-level study, which is academic and uses on-chain data but depends on how agent wallets are identified. Weakest: Olas aggregates, Lightning Rod's returns, Mantic's hedge-fund claim and viral posts.
- **"37% of agents profitable" is not evidence of forecasting edge.** It is a share of instances, not a capital-weighted return; it comes from the operator; and the comparison group includes the 69% of retail accounts that lose.
- **The commercial pattern in 2026 is selling forecasts, not trading them.** The best-funded forecasting startups (Mantic, FutureSearch, Cassi, Lightning Rod) publish benchmark results and sell access; none publishes a trading account. That is consistent with forecasting skill at about market level being worth more as a product than as a trading book.
- **AI-lab tie-ups so far are data-display deals** (odds shown inside chat or search products), not model-trading partnerships.
