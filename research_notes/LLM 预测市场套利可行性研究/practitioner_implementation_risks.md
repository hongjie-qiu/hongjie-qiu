## 1. Ecosystem: frameworks, SDKs, MCP servers, agent platforms, hosted products, data vendors (maturity, activity, security)

### Takeaway
Tooling is abundant but unstable and hostile: Polymarket archived its own LLM agent framework and its V1 clients in May 2026 and has shipped three generations of official client in about eight months, Kalshi's official surface is thin (auto-generated SDKs, no official MCP found), and the open-source layer around both is large (597 repos under the `kalshi` topic alone) but heavily seeded with malware (100 malware advisories mentioning "polymarket" in GitHub's advisory database). Nearly every serious open-source agent or MCP project ships dry-run defaults and loss disclaimers rather than performance evidence.

### Inferences
- Polymarket's own "AI agents" story has moved from a trading framework (archived, never updated after 2024) to documentation-as-skill files plus a CLI and SDKs. A builder in November 2026 should treat `Polymarket/agents` and most tutorials based on it (and on `py-clob-client`) as obsolete.
- The official `agent-skills` content predates the April 2026 V2/pUSD cutover, so an agent that follows it literally will produce V1-style orders that the exchange now rejects (see section 5 for the `order_version_mismatch` reports).
- Attackers explicitly target AI-agent builders: malicious package names include "ai-agent", "claude-code", "kelly-math" and "mcp-". One malicious npm name (`polymarket-toolkit`) coincides with a legitimate GitHub project name. An LLM coding assistant that "helpfully" installs a plausible package name is a realistic infection path.
- Kalshi's ecosystem is smaller and less attacked in the sources I saw; its official SDKs are generated code with a proprietary licence field and there is no first-party agent tooling.

### Gaps
- I could not open docs.polymarket.com, docs.kalshi.com or vendor pricing pages, so prices for sports-data vendors (Sportradar, OpticOdds, The Odds API), news APIs and commercial order-book vendors are missing.
- I did not verify whether any official Kalshi or Polymarket MCP server exists beyond what GitHub shows.
- StepSecurity, SafeDep, SlowMist and Koi reports were seen only as search summaries; package names, counts and dates should be checked against the originals.
- Maturity of the roughly 50 hosted "AI agent" products could not be assessed.

## 2. Verified outcomes: auditable track records versus claims and viral stories

### Takeaway
I found no LLM-driven agent with an independently auditable **positive** real-money record. The best-documented real-money results are negative or null: all six frontier models in the Prediction Arena study lost 16.0% to 30.8% of $10,000 on Kalshi in 57 days (per search summaries of the paper and its press coverage), and the only open-source operator I found who publishes a live account scores "NO MEASURED EDGE" with a net realised loss. Viral "my AI bot made X" stories were, where checked, uncorroborated, misattributed or marketing funnels.

## 3. Economics: cost stack versus edge and capacity for a small account

### Takeaway
Inference is no longer the binding cost: at November 2026 list prices a researched decision costs roughly $0.05–$0.60 and a full small-bot workload about $30–$360 a month. The binding constraints are taker fees of roughly 2–3.5% of notional near 50¢, spreads that one vendor puts at 2–5%, and the absence of demonstrated edge, so a $1,000 account typically cannot cover its fixed costs even if it had a real few-percent edge.

### Inferences
- **Cost per decision (my arithmetic from the list prices above).** Triage call (3,000 input + 300 output tokens): about $0.0005 (Haiku 5.5), $0.001 (gpt-5.6-luna), $0.009 (Sonnet 5.5). Deep-research call (40,000 input + 6,000 output tokens including reasoning): about $0.007 (Haiku 5.5), $0.015 (gpt-5.6-luna), $0.05 (gemini-3.8-flash), $0.14 (Sonnet 5.5), $0.28 (Opus 5.5 or gpt-5.6), $0.56 (Fable 5.2), $2.28 (gpt-5.5-pro). Add $0.005–$0.08 for five searches and $0.50 per 100 X posts read. A three-model ensemble triples the model line.
- **Monthly run cost for a small bot** scanning 200 markets a day with cheap triage and 20 deep dives: about $31/month with small models only, about $160/month with Sonnet-class research, about $360/month with Fable-class research. This is consistent with the practitioner data points ($10/day cap; $460/month).
- **Fees as a share of notional.** At p = 0.50 the Polymarket taker fee is 1.0¢ per share for politics (2.0% of the 50¢ price), 1.25¢ for sports/economics/weather (2.5%) and 1.75¢ for crypto (3.5%); at p = 0.95 politics costs about 0.19¢ (0.2%). The oracle3 example reproduces exactly under a 0.07 × contracts × P × (1 − P) Kalshi taker formula (1.68 + 1.7325 = $3.4125), i.e. 3.5% of notional at 50¢ and 68% of that example's gross edge.
- **Breakeven.** With fixed AI/data cost F per month, N trades, average stake S and net edge e (after fees and spread), breakeven needs N × S × e ≥ F. For F = $300, N = 100 and e = 3%, S must be at least $100; at quarter-Kelly-style stakes of 3–5% of bankroll that implies a bankroll of roughly $2,000–$3,300. At e = 1% it implies $6,000–$10,000. A $1,000 account staking $30 per trade earns $0.90 per trade at a 3% net edge, about $90 a month for 100 trades.
- The measured real-money results in section 2 show a *negative* realised edge (−1.9 points on one account; −16% to −31% in Prediction Arena), so on this evidence the first question for a builder is whether any edge exists, before cost recovery.
- Capacity is asymmetric: the places where a small account can still be filled (long-tail, low-volume markets) are those where exits are hardest (section 5).

### Gaps
- I found no practitioner-published breakeven analysis with real cost and P&L ledgers; the breakeven above is my own arithmetic.
- Infrastructure prices (VPS, RPC, monitoring) and per-market depth/capacity statistics were not retrieved.
- The post-Nov 20, 2026 Polymarket fee coefficients and Kalshi's current fee schedule were not opened (official docs unreachable).
- Prediction Arena's reported inference cost per model, if any, is unknown.

## 4. Architecture patterns that practitioners report as working or not working

### Takeaway
The pipeline shape is nearly universal (scan → triage → retrieve → estimate → edge threshold → fractional Kelly → guarded execution → settlement-based scoring). What separates credible projects is deterministic code around the LLM: the model reads rules, classifies text and filters candidates, while pricing, sizing, risk limits and order submission are hard-coded and often human-confirmed. Using the LLM as an independent pricer against a liquid book, trusting paper results, and trusting backtests that overlap the model's training window are the patterns reported as failing.

### Cited Findings

**Reference pipelines**
- [A] Polymarket's archived reference agent: fetch all tradeable events → RAG filter (Chroma) → map to markets → filter → "superforecaster" prompt → `one_best_trade` prompt → size parsed by regex from free text as a fraction of USDC balance (price parsing commented out, side not parsed) → execution commented out. — [executor.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Polymarket/agents/main/agents/application/executor.py); [trade.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Polymarket/agents/main/agents/application/trade.py)
- [A] Olas trader loop: retrieve open markets → pick one → ask an AI Mech for `p_yes`/`p_no` and confidence → bet only "if the bet clears the configured profitability threshold", otherwise blacklist the market (default cooldown 3,600 s) → redeem. Sizing is an "execution-aware" Kelly strategy with `min_edge`, bet-size bounds, `fee_per_trade` and an optional bid-ask spread band; each service also pins a "tools accuracy" hash for the Mech tools (the README does not explain how it is used). — [valory-xyz/trader](https://github.com/valory-xyz/trader)
- [C] `market-edge`: three loops (asynchronous research dossiers; daily strategy scan with a cheap screening model; 15-minute tactical loop with full analysis, sizing and execution) and a four-pass estimate (base rate without the price → Bayesian update on the dossier → adversarial pass → optional Brier calibration). — [market-edge](https://github.com/datori/market-edge)
- [C] OctagonAI CLI: scan every 60 minutes → cached research report → edge versus live order book → minimum edge 0.05 → half-Kelly → five risk gates (Kelly, liquidity, correlation, concentration, drawdown) → execution → calibration from settlements. — [kalshi-trading-bot-cli](https://github.com/OctagonAI/kalshi-trading-bot-cli)
- [C] ryanfrigo toolkit: ingest → decide (one LLM at temperature 0, or a no-LLM rule strategy) → optional adversarial verify (a "skeptic" tries to refute the trade, then a deterministic gate recomputes the edge from the live book) → risk governor → track → settle and score (Brier, log-loss, edge versus book) → "Edge Policy" that blocks categories with net losses over at least 5 settled trades and haircuts overconfident probability bands. — [README](https://github.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot)

**LLM for triage and reading versus LLM for the final price**
- [C] "It's a good text classifier. It's a bad pricer." The model compressed all 601 forecasts into 0.2–0.7, could not do threshold arithmetic ("It said 0.27 for a gas contract that needed a 49¢ crash in five days"), and produced mutually exclusive buckets summing to 1.67 and 2.55. — [JEV.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot/HEAD/docs/JEV.md)
- [C] The use that worked was filtering plus human review: confirming *absence* across thousands of transcripts to make base-rate fades cheap ("I filter with Jev, then read every hit myself"), and exact rule-text matching (16/16 on word-form rules such as "tariffs" versus "tariffed"). — [JEV.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot/HEAD/docs/JEV.md)
- [C] "The LLM is a calibration layer on top of the market price, not an independent forecaster... Without it, estimates are 3.5× worse." — [market-edge](https://github.com/datori/market-edge)
- [C] "Category discipline mattered more than AI confidence. The LLM could be 80% confident on a CPI trade and still be wrong. Market-implied probabilities on highly-watched economic releases are already efficient." — [ryanfrigo README](https://github.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot)
- [C] Anchoring cuts both ways: "Never put the market price in the state" when you want an independent estimate; the Octagon vendor admits its probability is "market-anchored". — [JEV.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot/HEAD/docs/JEV.md); [OctagonAI](https://github.com/OctagonAI/kalshi-trading-bot-cli)
- [C] Ensembles: the one project that advertised a "5-model ensemble" retracted it; real multi-model voting sits unwired in an `experimental/` folder. (snippet) Chinese coverage promotes multi-model cross-checking with Kelly sizing and daily loss caps, and notes in the same article that another OpenClaw-based agent was wiped out to zero (清算归零). — [ryanfrigo README](https://github.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot); [Odaily](https://www.odaily.news/zh-CN/post/5210096)

**Human-in-the-loop and guard rails**
- [A] Two-step prepare/confirm order tools, read-only defaults, environment-variable ceilings that the model cannot raise ("can tighten limits but cannot loosen them"), and cancel/decrease always allowed. — [kalshi-mcp-server README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cejor6/kalshi-mcp-server/HEAD/README.md)
- [A] Operator-set notional caps and market allow-lists checked in plain Python before anything is sent; but the deterministic `check_order` "only runs when your agent calls it". — [oddsrail](https://github.com/hmesutozsoy/oddsrail)
- [A] Preview-then-confirm with a default $50 per-buy cap. — [polyclaw](https://github.com/chainstacklabs/polyclaw)
- [C] Kill switches that actually halt: "A 50% drawdown limit isn't a limit. The default is 20% with the circuit breaker enforced in the order path... so it halts trades instead of just logging"; under a halt only reduce-only sells pass. — [ryanfrigo README](https://github.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot)
- [C] Sizing: "at a 45% win rate, three-quarter Kelly compounds losses catastrophically. Quarter-Kelly is what the example strategies use." — [ryanfrigo README](https://github.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot)

**Backtesting and paper trading pitfalls**
- [C] Training-window leakage: an apparent edge on earthquake-frequency markets was "primarily a contamination artifact: all 25 seismic markets in the backtest DB resolve in 2025, entirely within the LLM's training window. The edge monotonically decays and reverses past the training cutoff." — [market-edge](https://github.com/datori/market-edge)
- [C] Forward-only scoring: count "only trades where the event resolved strictly after you traded. Leakage and thin samples are quarantined"; refuse any verdict below 10 forward-settled trades. The Jev test used only markets that settled after the model's build date. — [ryanfrigo README](https://github.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot); [JEV.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot/HEAD/docs/JEV.md)
- [C] Paper-to-live gap: +77% on paper versus −74% live for the same logic; 18.3% of the author's own trade records wrong, two thirds of them false wins. — [polymarket-toolkit README](https://github.com/runesleo/polymarket-toolkit)
- [C] Fill assumptions: Simmer's backtests "use trade-tape prices (no orderbook), so they model decision quality, not execution realism"; oddsrail calls paper fills "a best case". Homerun argues that the usual "depth-ahead-must-clear" heuristic is inadequate and advertises L2 replay (25 levels, 0.5 s sampling), a fitted fill-probability model and backtest/shadow/live "triangulation" (vendor claims, not verified). — [simmer-sdk README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/SpartanLabsXyz/simmer-sdk/HEAD/README.md); [oddsrail](https://github.com/hmesutozsoy/oddsrail); [homerun README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/braedonsaunders/homerun/HEAD/README.md)
- [A] Fee and rebate modelling: the backtest fee model approximates maker rebates because a backtest "does not know the total fee equivalent from other makers"; it does not model maker competition, daily aggregation or minimum payout thresholds; and "Commission is an estimate from the instrument's fee metadata, not a venue-reported charge". — [NautilusTrader Polymarket doc](https://github.com/nautechsystems/nautilus_trader/blob/develop/docs/integrations/polymarket.md)
- [C] (snippet) Chinese warning that advertised returns are mostly backtests that ignore fees, slippage and liquidity. — [Zhihu](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/2012564209100136790)

### Inferences
- The designs that survive contact with real money put the LLM *upstream* (what to look at, what the rule says, whether a text satisfies it) and keep price, size and send decisions in deterministic code with hard caps.
- A graduated path is the de facto standard: backtest → paper on live prices → shadow or "tiny-live" → small live, with settlement-based scoring before any scale-up.
- Self-improvement loops keyed to settled outcomes (category blocks, calibration haircuts) are a cheap way to stop repeating losses, but with 5–55 trades they are statistically weak and can overfit.
- Multi-model ensembles are widely advertised and rarely evidenced; I found no settled-trade comparison of ensemble versus single-model agents.

### Gaps
- No controlled, real-money comparison of architectures (triage-only versus final-decision, ensemble versus single model, human-in-the-loop versus autonomous) was found.
- Hacker News, Reddit and Substack post-mortems were not searched.
- Academic work on leakage and benchmark design is covered by another researcher and was not opened here.

## 5. Failure modes and risks, with concrete incidents

### Takeaway
The failures documented in 2026 are mostly operational and adversarial, not exotic AI failures: platform migrations that silently broke bots, "matched" orders that never settled, retries that doubled positions, key-stealing packages, compromised front ends and bait attacks on predictable bots. LLM-specific failures (overconfidence, arithmetic errors, misread rules) are well evidenced in practitioner logs, while prompt injection against a trading agent is a credible design-level risk for which I found no documented prediction-market incident.

### Cited Findings

**Hallucination, miscalibration and misread rules**
- [C] Live overconfidence: 73% predicted versus 44% realised (n=16) and 92% versus 71% (n=14) on one Kalshi account. — [TRACK_RECORD.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot/HEAD/docs/TRACK_RECORD.md)
- [C] Arithmetic and state errors: a model "gave 0.62-0.92 on weather markets that had already settled". — [JEV.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot/HEAD/docs/JEV.md)
- [C] Rules matter more than the model: "The best trade of the weekend came from reading a rule, not from any model: OpenRouter share markets settle all-NO if an author isn't named in the top-9 chart... Read the rules." (The cited position was still open; outcome unverified.) — [JEV.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ryanfrigo/kalshi-ai-trading-bot/HEAD/docs/JEV.md)

### Inferences
- An autonomous agent multiplies these operational faults: a ghost fill or a 5-second timeout becomes a double position when an LLM (or a naive loop) "helpfully" retries. Idempotency, reconciliation against on-chain balances and "unknown outcome" states must be coded outside the model.
- Prompt injection is most dangerous where the same agent both reads the open web (or market comments) and holds signing authority. The only strong mitigations in the sources are architectural: a reader agent without tools, allow-listed data sources, hard caps and human confirmation.
- Predictable bots are prey. Both documented attacks exploited bots that reacted mechanically to book state or reward rules; an LLM agent with a fixed, discoverable policy is exposed in the same way.
- Capital can be locked by layers the agent does not model: dispute escalation (days), venue balance bugs (minutes to hours), trading halts after front-end compromises.
- The two 2026 front-end compromises were described as malicious scripts in the website, so a headless bot that never loads the site was presumably not the target; such a bot remains exposed to package malware, key theft on its own host and the trading halts that follow an incident.

### Gaps
- **Not researched (search budget exhausted):** 2026 oracle/resolution disputes and manipulation incidents, fake-news and manipulated-source incidents, account bans, and any prompt-injection incident through market descriptions or comment sections. I found no documented case of the last kind, which is not evidence that none exists.
- Leads from my pre-2026 background knowledge that I could **not** re-verify this session and that need sources before use: the March 2025 Ukraine mineral-deal market resolved after a large UMA holder's vote; the mid-2025 "Zelensky suit" resolution dispute; UMA's 2025 move to whitelisted proposers for Polymarket markets.
- Incident details from security firms and Chinese media are search-summary level; the reported loss figures for the June and October 2026 Polymarket compromises conflict across outlets.
- Kalshi-side operational incidents (outages, API changes, ghost fills) were not found; Kalshi has no public issue tracker comparable to Polymarket's.

## 6. Legal and terms-of-service constraints on automated trading and AI agents; platform policies toward bots

### Takeaway
Both venues accommodate API and bot trading in practice (official SDKs, rate-limit tiers, maker incentives, an official Polymarket CLI and agent "skills"), and I found no rule aimed specifically at AI agents; the binding constraints are who may trade (jurisdiction and account eligibility) and whose account an agent may trade for. I could not open the current terms of either venue, so this section rests on statements in official repositories and third-party paraphrases.

- [A] Polymarket's (now archived) official agent framework states: "[Terms of Service](https://polymarket.com/tos) prohibit US persons and persons from certain other jurisdictions from trading on Polymarket (via UI & API and including agents developed by persons in restricted jurisdictions), although data and information is viewable globally." ### Inferences
- The practical legal question for an AI agent is identity, not automation: the account holder is responsible for every order the agent sends, and running an agent on someone else's account or pooling users behind one account is where hosted products hit venue terms (hence paper-only hosted modes and "bring your own keys").
- Eligibility checks happen at order time, so a bot can read data, pass back-tests and paper trading, and then be rejected live. Geo-eligibility has to be established before building, and nothing in an agent framework substitutes for it.
- Because Polymarket international and Polymarket US are distinct venues with different APIs, keys and SDKs, code and agents are not portable between them without rework.

### Gaps
- The current Polymarket (international and US) terms, the Kalshi rulebook and the Kalshi API Developer Agreement were not opened; any statement about what they permit for bots or AI agents needs direct verification. Another researcher covers platform rules and regulation in detail.
- I found no venue statement specifically about AI agents (disclosure, liability, market-manipulation rules applied to autonomous systems) and no enforcement case against an AI-agent operator.
- The CFTC advisory on AI trading-bot fraud was referenced in a search summary only.

## 7. Practitioner consensus in 2026: where LLM agents add real edge versus hype

### Takeaway
Among practitioners who publish settled results there is rough agreement: an LLM is a useful reader, classifier, research assistant and code-writing accelerator, and at best a calibration layer on top of market prices; it is not an independent source of pricing edge against liquid books, and the profits that are real come from execution, speed, structure and rule-reading. Optimistic claims in 2026 come mainly from platform teams and marketers and rest on team-supplied or unaudited numbers.

### Inferences
- The consensus among measurers is narrower than the hype but not nihilistic: LLMs lower the cost of *finding and reading* (rules, texts, thin markets nobody watches) and of *building*, and those gains matter most in slow, text-resolved, low-attention markets where speed is not decisive.
- A realistic builder's edge thesis in late 2026 is therefore "rule-reading and coverage of neglected markets with strict risk code", with the LLM as a component, and the default expectation, on the evidence in section 2, is no measurable edge until at least dozens of forward-settled trades say otherwise.
- Survivorship and incentive bias run through the optimistic sources: platform teams report their own agents, course sellers and affiliates profit from sign-ups, and several "honest" repos also sell write-ups or carry referral links.

### Gaps
- Mainstream financial press (Bloomberg, WSJ, FT), Wired, The Block and named-trader commentary on X were not searched or opened; the "consensus" here is drawn from open-source practitioners, one academic study seen via summaries, and Chinese community write-ups.
- No survey or systematic sample of practitioners exists in the sources I saw; the sample is small and skewed toward people who publish.
- Views of professional market makers and prop firms using LLMs internally are absent.
