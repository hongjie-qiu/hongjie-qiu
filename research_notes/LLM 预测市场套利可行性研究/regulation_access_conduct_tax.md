# Legal and regulatory conditions for an individual trading real-money prediction markets (预测市场) with automated or LLM-driven strategies: access, platform terms, prohibited conduct, enforcement, tax and fund protection — state as of 26 November 2026

How to read these notes. Every finding carries two labels.

- Evidence grade: **[P]** primary or official (platform terms, rulebook, regulator or court record, company statement on its own site or repository); **[L]** law-firm, CRS or bar-association analysis; **[N]** reporting by an established news outlet; **[S]** secondary guide, tracker, aggregator or affiliate site; **[U]** unverified or headline only.
- Access: **(full)** = the document text was read in this session; **(snip)** = only a search-result summary was seen. Page fetching was blocked for almost every host. The exceptions that could be read in full were GitHub-hosted files (including third-party mirrors of `docs.polymarket.com`) and Kalshi's public document bucket (`kalshi-public-docs.s3.amazonaws.com`).

Dates are given for each fact. Anything dated before mid-2026 should be treated as possibly superseded. These notes describe restrictions; they contain no guidance on getting around geoblocking, KYC or platform rules. Nothing here is legal or tax advice.

## 1. Polymarket international: restricted jurisdictions and tiers, KYC, terms on restricted persons, government actions, and the exposure of individual users

### Takeaway
By late 2026 Polymarket's own documentation sorted restricted places into three groups: seven OFAC-sanctioned jurisdictions that are blocked completely (positions cannot even be closed), about 33 countries plus four Canadian provinces that are close-only on both website and API (the US, UK, France, Germany, Italy, Belgium, Poland, Australia, Singapore, Taiwan and Thailand among them), and a website-only close-only group (Ireland, Japan, Netherlands, Malta for sports, and South Korea by October 2026). Roughly 30 or more governments have blocked or warned against the site, and individual users, not only the operator, have been pursued in Taiwan (election-betting prosecutions, 2024 and 2026) and South Korea (26 users booked, September 2026); Singapore's statute and Hong Kong's regulator both say users themselves can commit an offence, and mainland-China commentary treats participation as gambling, with no reported user case found.

**C. What the terms say about restricted persons**

- [P](full; repository last pushed Nov 2024, possibly superseded) Polymarket's official `agents` repository states: "Terms of Service prohibit US persons and persons from certain other jurisdictions from trading on Polymarket (via UI & API and including agents developed by persons in restricted jurisdictions), although data and information is viewable globally." — [Polymarket/agents README](https://github.com/Polymarket/agents/blob/main/README.md)
- [P](snip) Polymarket's transparency page says users in restricted jurisdictions, including the United States, are prohibited from trading on Polymarket International "or using tools intended to circumvent geographic restrictions", and that Terms of Use violations are referred to law enforcement or regulators when appropriate. — [Polymarket transparency page](https://polymarket.com/transparency)
- [N](snip; 2024, older) The VPN prohibition dates from the January 2022 CFTC settlement, which required blocking US-based traders; the terms were amended to prohibit using a VPN to get around the block. — [BNN Bloomberg, 1 Aug 2024](https://bnnbloomberg.ca/investing/2024/08/01/traders-defying-us-ban-embrace-polymarket-with-election-bets)

### Inferences
- Polymarket's own list and the list of governments that have acted are different things. A country's absence from the geoblock page (mainland China, Hong Kong, Spain, Switzerland, Portugal, Hungary, India) says nothing about local legality, and the frontend-only tier is a statement about Polymarket's controls, not about local law: South Korea prosecuted users in the same months it sat in, or was about to join, that tier.
- Two 2026 enforcement patterns bear directly on an automated trader. First, police in Taiwan and South Korea identified users from the public ledger, so pseudonymous on-chain trading did not prevent attribution. Second, the cases turned on where the person was when the bet was placed (Taiwan) or on residence (South Korea), not on where the platform is hosted.
- For a resident of mainland China none of the three main venues offers a route that is clearly permitted by both the platform and local law: Kalshi's agreement bars event-contract trading from the PRC, Polymarket US is reported to require US identity documents (section 2), and Polymarket international, although it does not self-restrict China, is described by mainland commentary as gambling with an added crypto-settlement problem. The same holds in substance for Taiwan and Singapore (close-only on Polymarket, restricted on Kalshi, user offences on the books).
- The OFAC tier shows the worst case of a tier change: positions cannot be closed at all. A country moving into a close-only tier (as happened to several in mid-2026) forces a strategy into exit-only mode at whatever prices the book offers.
- The KYC position on the international venue is unsettled rather than relaxed: the company denied mandatory KYC in May 2026 while press reports described targeted verification and VPN-linked bans, and the co-location path already requires KYC/KYB.

## 2. Polymarket US and Kalshi: who can open an account, state availability, Kalshi abroad, KYC, state-versus-federal litigation, legislation and CFTC rulemaking

### Takeaway
Both US venues are CFTC-regulated and identity-verified, but availability is now fractured by state: by November 2026 four federal appeals courts had ruled (Third Circuit for Kalshi; Ninth, Sixth and, on 18 November, Fourth Circuit for the states), a certiorari petition (Flaherty v. KalshiEX, No. 26-299) was pending without a grant, and state orders had forced geofencing in Nevada, Michigan, Washington and, for ten days from 12 November, a complete lock-out of Louisiana members. Kalshi's member agreement of 1 October 2026 bars event-contract trading from 55 jurisdictions; no federal statute on event contracts had been enacted, and the CFTC's 2026 rules were still proposals apart from an interim final rule and staff guidance.

### Inferences
- State availability is the least stable parameter of the US venues. In four months (August–November 2026) access changed by court order or settlement in at least Nevada, Michigan, Washington and Louisiana, and three appellate rulings opened Ohio, Tennessee, Maryland and the Ninth Circuit states to enforcement. A strategy that depends on sports or election contracts on Kalshi or Polymarket US is exposed to sudden, state-specific loss of market access.
- The Louisiana episode is the worst-case template: an ex parte order took effect at once and, on Kalshi's own reading, required blocking even closing orders. An automated system holding positions for a resident of an affected state could neither hedge nor exit on that venue while the order stood.
- Federal and state instructions now pull in opposite directions (the CFTC telling exchanges to drop state geofences within 30 days from early November 2026; state courts ordering geofences). Until the Supreme Court acts, which on the reported timetable may not be before the 2027–28 term, exchanges will keep resolving this conflict state by state.
- Kalshi's restricted list grew from 45 to 55 jurisdictions in a year, tracking foreign regulator actions; its "140 countries" expansion and its restricted list should be read together, and the member agreement, not marketing, is the governing text.

## 3. Terms governing automated trading: bots, AI agents, multiple accounts, wash and self-trading, spoofing, and use of data

### Takeaway
Automated trading through the official APIs is contemplated by all three venues, but under conduct rules that are the same as for manual trading and under data terms that are narrower than many bot builders assume. Kalshi's documents, read in full, limit API use to a member's own trading, allow one account per member, prohibit wash, pre-arranged and spoofing trades, forbid sharing API data with third parties, and contain a data-terms clause expressly prohibiting use of Kalshi Data with machine learning or AI without written consent. For Polymarket only fragments were readable: the terms prohibit wash trading, spoofing, self-dealing and trading by restricted persons including through agents, and incentive programmes can be clawed back for self-matching or linked accounts.

## 4. Insider trading and manipulation: legal basis, status of the 2026 cases, new cases, legislation, and the exposure of someone who copies a suspected insider

### Takeaway
US authorities prosecute event-contract insider trading under the Commodity Exchange Act's anti-fraud provision and CFTC Rule 180.1 (misappropriation), the "Eddie Murphy" provision on government information, and wire fraud; the first criminal trial (Van Dyke) was set for 7 December 2026 and no plea, verdict or sentence was found in either criminal case. Since mid-2026 the visible activity has been civil and exchange-level: CFTC settlements with George Santos (late July) and a teleprompter operator (about $172,500, late August) and six Kalshi disciplinary notices effective 28 August 2026; no new criminal case, no enacted statute and no commentary on follower liability was found.

### Cited Findings

**A. Legal basis**

- [L](snip) CFTC Rule 180.1, modelled on SEC Rule 10b-5, is the main civil theory: trading event contracts on misappropriated material non-public information is fraud in connection with a swap or commodity contract. ### Inferences
- Every US case so far targets a person who held and misused confidential information or could influence the outcome. None targets a trader who merely reacted to public order flow. That is an observation about cases brought, not a safe harbour: the theory that a follower who knows the source is misappropriated information is a "tippee" has simply not been tested for event contracts.
- The facts that would matter if it were tested are knowledge and connection: a private tip, coordination with the insider, or a tool marketed and used for "tailing insiders" would make intent easier to allege, while trading on a wallet's public on-chain activity with no link to the source resembles ordinary reaction to market information. This reasoning is the note-taker's, not a sourced legal view.
- The sanctions scale matters for sizing risk. Kalshi's penalties run from a few thousand dollars to permanent bans, the CFTC's civil orders in mid-2026 were in the tens to low hundreds of thousands of dollars with three-year trading bans, and the criminal cases involve profits of $0.4–1.2 million. Kalshi's Rule 5.17(z) threshold is very low ("no matter the scale and importance of the influence"), and purchases under $1,000 were sanctioned.
- Exchange surveillance is now a routine source of CFTC cases (the teleprompter case began with Kalshi's own flag), and Polymarket's referrals to DOJ are documented in the earlier round; platform-level detection should be assumed on both venues.

### Gaps
- Outcome of the Van Dyke trial (scheduled for 7 Dec 2026, after the date of these notes), any plea in either criminal case, and the reported mid-November evidentiary hearing in Spagnuolo were not retrieved. The Israeli prosecution's outcome was not researched further.
- The CFTC's own orders (Santos, Perez) were not read; the Santos CFTC terms and date remain as reported by secondary sources in the earlier round.
- No authority or commentary on tippee, aiding-and-abetting or follower liability for event contracts; no non-US regulator position on copy trading (跟单) of suspected insiders.
- Whether Polymarket (either venue) has published disciplinary outcomes comparable to Kalshi's notices is unknown.

## 5. Tax: how US taxpayers are expected to report prediction-market gains, and notes on other jurisdictions

### Takeaway
As of the latest reporting found (July 2026, with guides through October 2026 saying the same) the IRS has issued no guidance specific to event contracts, so practitioners choose between Section 1256 treatment, capital gain or loss, ordinary income, and gambling treatment, with most doubting Section 1256 and treating sports contracts as the most likely to be gambling. Form practice is inconsistent: Kalshi issues several 1099 forms but, on most accounts, none that fully reports event-contract profit; the international Polymarket venue issues none; and reports conflict on Polymarket US. Gains are taxable whether or not a form arrives.

### Cited Findings

- [N](snip) "The IRS has not issued specific guidance on the taxation of prediction market contracts as of July 2026" (Thomson Reuters, at the start of the World Cup); a second Thomson Reuters item says prediction-market income is taxable with or without forms. No notice, ruling or form instruction dated later was found. — [Thomson Reuters, Jul 2026](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/news/irs-silence-on-prediction-market-winnings-to-cause-confusion-as-world-cup-begins/); [Thomson Reuters](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/news/nil-prediction-market-income-taxable-with-or-without-forms-tax-pros-say/); [CDC Gaming](https://cdcgaming.com/brief/how-are-prediction-markets-taxed-the-irs-hasnt-provided-guidance-yet/)
- [L](snip) Competing treatments discussed by practitioners:
  - *Section 1256 contract* (mark-to-market, 60% long-term and 40% short-term, Form 6781): an ABA Tax Section panel discussed it; one practitioner reports a "collective view" that event contracts are unlikely to qualify, mainly because most lack daily mark-to-market margining; White & Case says some contracts "may qualify as regulated futures contracts"; Seward & Kissel devotes a memo to the question.
  - *Gambling*: sports-referencing contracts "could potentially be viewed as gambling activities"; losses would then be deductible only against winnings.

  - How the wash-sale rules apply is unresolved.

### Inferences
- For a US taxpayer the classification choice changes results materially for an active automated strategy with many winning and losing contracts: gambling treatment limits loss use, Section 1256 would give blended rates and year-end mark-to-market, and ordinary-income reporting via 1099-MISC gives neither. Because no guidance exists, the defensible course practitioners describe is consistent treatment, full trade records and disclosure of the position taken.
- Record-keeping falls on the trader on every venue: Kalshi's forms are partial, Polymarket US practice is unclear, and the offshore venue provides nothing and layers stablecoin basis tracking on top.
- In jurisdictions where the activity is classed as unlicensed gambling (most of section 1), tax classification is secondary to legality; tax-free "gambling winnings" treatment in a country such as the UK presupposes a lawful product.

### Gaps
- No IRS or Treasury document was read; whether guidance appeared between August and November 2026, or in 2026 form instructions, is unknown.
- Kalshi's and Polymarket US's own tax help pages and the participant agreement were not read; the form lists and thresholds above are from third-party guides that conflict.

- No reliable source was found for mainland China, Hong Kong, Singapore or Taiwan tax treatment of such winnings; no practitioner source on whether swap (notional principal contract) treatment is being used.

## 6. Customer-fund protection: what happens to funds if a venue fails or is shut down

### Takeaway
On Kalshi, retail members' cash sits with the affiliated clearing house, Kalshi Klear, under rules that require segregation from the company's own property and conservative investment, but members are "self-clearing" and their deposits are classed as proprietary funds held in an omnibus settlement account at one bank; FDIC cover is expressly "not guaranteed", the clearing house disclaims liability for a bank failure, and if the clearing house itself fails or winds up, positions are closed out at a termination price. On the international Polymarket venue users hold pUSD and outcome tokens in their own smart wallets with no intermediary, insurer or regulator standing behind them; the risks are contract, key and administrative-pause risk, and loss of access by jurisdiction.

### Cited Findings

**A. Kalshi and Kalshi Klear (documents read in full)**

- [P](full; Kalshi Klear DCO Rules v1.6, file dated 4 Nov 2026) Rule 7.5: fully collateralised contracts must be fully collateralised at all times. Rule 7.7: "Participants' collateral shall be legally and operationally segregated from the property of the Company"; funds deposited by a self-clearing member "shall be segregated from all other funds held by the Company and treated as belonging to Members"; customers of futures commission merchants get the CFTC Regulation 1.20 or Part 22 customer-segregated account treatment. — [Kalshi Klear DCO Rules v1.6](https://kalshi-public-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/regulatory/rulebook/DCO%20Rulebook%201.6.docx.pdf)
- [P](full) Rule 7.9 of the same rules: participant funds may be invested only under CFTC Regulations 1.25, 22.1 and 39.15, "exclusively in U.S. Treasury securities and U.S. government agency securities, reverse repurchase agreements for these securities, and U.S. government money market mutual funds", convertible to cash within one business day, maximum maturity two years, held at US banks, and "will not, under any circumstances, be commingled with the Company's funds"; Klear keeps investment profit not paid to members as interest. — [Kalshi Klear DCO Rules v1.6](https://kalshi-public-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/regulatory/rulebook/DCO%20Rulebook%201.6.docx.pdf)
- [P](full; KalshiEX Rulebook v1.32) Rule 3.1(e): "All funds deposited by Self-Clearing Members shall constitute 'Proprietary Funds' in accordance with CFTC Regulations." ### Inferences
- The three venues fail in different ways. Kalshi: legal segregation and conservative investment, but concentration in one settlement bank with the bank-failure risk contractually left with members, proprietary-funds status rather than the FCM-customer regime, and a close-out at a termination price if the clearing house fails. Polymarket US: an FCM-customer structure on paper, with insolvency treatment not verified. Polymarket international: no counterparty holding the money, but also no insurer, no regulator and an administrator-controlled pause on converting pUSD back to USDC.
- "Non-custodial" on the international venue describes who holds the keys, not what the user can do in a crisis. Exits still depend on Polymarket's matching engine, on the oracle for resolution, on the off-ramp contract being unpaused and on the user's jurisdiction not being moved into a blocked tier.
- For open positions, the forced-close or frozen-access scenarios documented in 2026 (termination-price close-out rules, the Louisiana no-orders rule, close-only tiers) are more likely to cost a trader money than an outright loss of cash balances, because marks at forced exit can differ sharply from expected value at resolution.

### Gaps
- No legal analysis was found of how Part 190 would treat self-clearing members' "proprietary funds" at Kalshi Klear in an insolvency, and the DCO default waterfall (Rule 12.5) was not reviewed in detail.
- The Polymarket US and QC Clearing rulebooks and the FCM customer agreement were not read; fund protection there rests on search summaries.
- For the international venue, the identity and powers of the pUSD administrator, upgradeability of the contracts and any published audit were not examined; no documented case of pUSD wrapping or unwrapping being paused was found.
- No source describes what happened to user balances in countries that moved to close-only or blocked status in 2026, or whether any account balances have been frozen on the international venue.
