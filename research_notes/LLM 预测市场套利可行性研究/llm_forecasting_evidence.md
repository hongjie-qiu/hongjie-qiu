## 1. Benchmarks and tournaments: latest results, model versions, market baseline, trend, and whether anyone has beaten the market out of sample

### Takeaway
During 2026 the best LLM systems reached approximate, statistically indistinguishable parity with elite humans (ForecastBench superforecasters; Metaculus Pros), and bots took 1st and 2nd in the Summer 2026 Metaculus Cup. Against the *market price*, however, the evidence is neutral to negative: on market-derived questions most systems do no better than copying the price they are shown, Prophet Arena models roughly tie the Kalshi baseline on Brier while failing to break even on returns, and in real-money or order-book-realistic trading tests most LLM agents lose money; the one clearly positive live result found (a 26-day Kalshi run, +80.33% ROI) is short, self-reported and unreplicated.

#### Prophet Arena (University of Chicago; events drawn from Kalshi)
- [A] "LLM-as-a-Prophet" (ICLR 2026): live benchmark; more than 1,300 resolved events; scores are Brier, calibration and simulated market return. Frontier models (GPT-5, Grok-4, Claude Sonnet 4) reached Brier 0.18–0.22 against a market baseline of 0.187 (0.188 in another version) and random 0.25; all model scores sit in a narrow band [0.17, 0.24]. All selected LLMs were better calibrated than the market baseline (top models ECE ≤ 0.05 vs market ECE 0.069). Returns showed large variance, "with even the top-ranked GPT-5 failing to reach break-even (Average Return < 1)". — [arXiv 2510.17638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638); [ICLR 2026 proceedings PDF](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/file/aec5e2847c5ae90f939ab786774856cc-Paper-Conference.pdf)
- [A] Same paper, input ablation (model not named in the snippet): Brier 0.169 with both market data and news sources; 0.173 with market data only; 0.191 with sources only; 0.235 with neither. Some LLMs beat the market baseline at longer horizons, but "the market adjusts rapidly to new signals and achieves the highest short-term accuracy near event resolution". Most detailed results come from a five-model subset of 23 evaluated models. — [arXiv 2510.17638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638)
- [C, conflicting summaries of the same paper] One summary says frontier models "performed similarly to the market baseline"; another says GPT-5 (reasoning) and Grok 4 "consistently outperformed the Market Baseline in terms of accuracy", with Gemini 2.5 Flash (Thinking) second in five of six rankings. — [papernotes summary](https://en.papernotes.org/ICLR2026/llm_evaluation/llm-as-a-prophet_understanding_predictive_intelligence_with_prophet_arena/)
- [C, August 2025, superseded] Early leaderboard coverage: GPT-5 led on Brier ("82.21%", i.e. 1 − Brier); o3-mini led on returns, largely through one underdog bet (about $1 → $9 on a Toronto FC match). — [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/336298/ai-now-matches-prediction-markets-in-forecasting-real-events-study-finds); [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/can-ai-predict-future/)

#### Real-money and order-book-realistic LLM trading tests
- [B] "When do prophets profit in prediction markets?" (Gu, Kagan, Sun, Wu, Xu; arXiv July 2026; Prophet Arena team): "every agent on the AI leaderboards that turn forecasts into prediction-market trades loses money, even though several beat the market under standard proper scoring rules"; simple strategies can earn positive expected profit even when the forecaster has no accuracy edge. The authors derive a "proper betting" strategy from the scoring rule; in a backtest over AI forecasts on 2,418 resolved Kalshi markets with historical bid/ask prices, only proper betting (vs max-margin, inverse-margin, Kelly and Kelly-like sizing) routinely converted out-of-sample accuracy into profit. — [arXiv 2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06166); [Prophet Arena research page](https://www.prophetarena.co/research/when-do-prophets-profit)
- [B, the strongest positive live result found] Same paper, live test: a Gemini 3 agent trading the Brier-proper strategy with real capital on Kalshi on a fixed two-hour cadence for 26 days, facing real spreads and fees, returned +80.33% ROI with a Sharpe ratio of 3.35. Starting capital and number of bets were not found. — [Prophet Arena research page](https://www.prophetarena.co/research/when-do-prophets-profit); [arXiv 2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06166)
- [C] Third-party review of that live test: eligibility filters (2–14 day horizon, ≥10¢ move, category exclusions) decide which Kalshi markets enter the book "and therefore the reported +80.33% ROI"; the live run does not measure or bound realised slippage. — [Pith Review](https://pith.science/paper/2607.06166)
- [B] Prediction Arena (Zhang et al.; arXiv, submitted 28 March 2026): models place real trades on Kalshi and Polymarket; evaluation 12 January – 9 March 2026 (57 days). Cohort 1 models averaged −22.6% on Kalshi vs −1.1% on Polymarket; grok-4-20-checkpoint had the highest settlement win rate, 71.4%. — [arXiv 2604.07355](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07355)
- [C, secondary summary of the same paper] On Kalshi, where all models trade the same questions, all six live-trading models lost money; final returns ranged from about −16% to −31%. A "next-gen" cohort ran only 3 days and used paper trading, so it is not comparable. — [Emergent Mind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2604.07355)
- [B] PolyBench (Cheng, Liu, Long; arXiv, 3 April 2026): 38,666 binary Polymarket markets across 4,997 events; seven LLMs made 36,165 predictions on timestamp-locked market states collected 6–12 February 2026; metrics include a Confidence-Weighted Return, annualised yield and Sharpe computed with simulated order-book execution. — [arXiv 2604.14199](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14199)
- [C, secondary write-up; the paper's table was not opened] PolyBench outcome: only two of seven models had a positive confidence-weighted return; the five losers reported average confidence between 0.808 and 0.871; Grok made about 33% on five contrarian bets ("an anecdote with a percentage sign attached"). — [Level Up Coding write-up](https://levelup.gitconnected.com/seven-ai-models-traded-prediction-markets-for-a-week-five-lost-money-111f228c6e6c)
- [B] Foresight Arena (Nechepurenko, Shuvalov; arXiv, May 2026): on-chain commit–reveal benchmark on Polygon; agents forecast binary Polymarket markets; scored by Brier and Murphy decomposition; 50-round live test of five frontier LLM agents. The authors argue trading PnL is a weaker metric because it mixes forecasting accuracy with market-timing and risk choices. Numeric results were not retrieved. — [arXiv 2605.00420](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.00420)
- [C/D] BallotBench (Thoughtful Lab; 2026 US midterms): nine agents each got $50 for real-money Kalshi trades plus simulated $10,000 Polymarket and Kalshi portfolios; small gains were reported for two real-money accounts after 24 hours, while a dated display showed all nine simulated portfolios below starting value; the project's homepage calls itself a synthetic preview. — [Digg summary](https://digg.com/ai/hqtqjfb1)
- [B, adjacent domain: sports betting, not a prediction-market venue] KellyBench (published at gr.inc, which I believe is the company General Reasoning; arXiv, April 2026; press says eight models were tried): models build betting strategies for the 2023/24 English Premier League season. Every model lost money on average across five seeds; mean final bankrolls ranged from £10,421 (Kimi K2.5) to £96,336 (Claude Opus 4.7) on a normalised £100,000 start; GPT-5.4 averaged −7.9%; a human quant made +5.1% ROI; a simple Dixon–Coles model "beats 3/5 of the models evaluated". Sources disagree on the best model (paper: Claude Opus 4.6; company release: Claude Opus 4.7; Decrypt: best model £89,035). — [arXiv 2604.27865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27865); [General Reasoning release](https://www.gr.inc/releases/introducing-kellybench); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/364406/ai-beat-sports-betting-market-top-8-models)
- [C] Bloomberg, 6 May 2026: public AI trading contests (example: Alpha Arena, run by Nof1) show leading models struggling to beat markets consistently; the arenas "run for only short periods" and are "far from academically rigorous". — [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-06/ai-bots-auditioning-for-wall-street-trading-are-mostly-losing)

#### FutureX (live agent benchmark; not a market-price comparison)

### Inferences
- **The gap to elite humans closed in 2026.** ForecastBench: 0.020 Brier (GPT-4.5 era) → 0.017 (January 2026) → Brier Index 67.9 vs 70.6 (March 2026) → 69.2 vs 68.8 (September 2026). Metaculus: −20.0 (Q2 2025) → −1.25 (Spring 2026) → −0.26 (Summer 2026). Both operators describe the 2026 results as statistical ties, not AI wins.
- **Brier Index conversions (my arithmetic, using Brier = (1 − BI/100)²):** 70.6 ↔ 0.086; 67.9 ↔ 0.103; 69.2 ↔ 0.095; 68.8 ↔ 0.097; 80.3 ↔ 0.039; 75.8 ↔ 0.059; copier line 70.93 ↔ 0.085. The September 2026 AI-vs-superforecaster difference is therefore about 0.002 Brier points.
- **"Parity with superforecasters" is not "beats the market".** The only analyses that test LLMs against the price they were shown (Copier Line; vaticinus/vati) find no demonstrable edge, and Good Judgment independently reports price-copying behaviour. The name of the top ForecastBench entry ("ensemble_2_crowdadj") suggests a crowd/market-adjusted ensemble; I could not confirm its method.
- **Prophet Arena's ablation implies the models' accuracy is largely inherited from the market price.** Without market data the model scored 0.191, worse than the market's 0.187; its best score (0.169) required the market price as an input. A forecaster whose edge disappears when the price is hidden has not shown an independent estimate that is better than the market.
- **The more realistic the execution, the worse the results.** Equal-stake simulated returns: below break-even (Prophet Arena). Order-book simulation: 5 of 7 models negative (PolyBench). Real money: −16% to −31% on Kalshi over 57 days (Prediction Arena). The one positive real-money result depends on a specific sizing rule and market filters, over 26 days.
- **Answer to "has any system demonstrably beaten the market price out of sample?"** Not demonstrably, as of November 2026. Candidates are suggestive only: "several" leaderboard agents beat the market under proper scoring rules (magnitude and significance not retrieved); the 26-day Kalshi run; Apodex's n = 70 claim; AIA Forecaster adding information when blended with the market (see KQ2).
- The absolute scores in the Copier Line essay (copier ≈ 70.9) and in Good Judgment's post (superforecasters 80.3, best AI 75.8 on market questions) cannot both be on the same question set and adjustment; they come from different analyses and dates and should not be compared directly.

## 2. Academic literature 2023–2026: methods, headline results, stated limitations

### Takeaway
The literature moved from "LLM systems trail the human crowd" (2024) to "scaffolded, ensembled or fine-tuned LLM systems match strong human aggregates" (2025–2026). But nearly every paper that includes a liquid-market baseline finds the market at least as accurate as the standalone LLM, and papers that test trading mostly report losses or small hypothetical gains.

### Cited Findings

#### Retrieval-augmented and agentic forecasters
- [A, 2024, superseded as a capability estimate] Halawi et al., "Approaching Human-Level Forecasting with Language Models" (NeurIPS 2024): retrieval-augmented system; 914 test questions opened after the models' knowledge cutoffs; system Brier 0.179 vs human crowd 0.149; accuracy 71.5% vs 77.0%; RMS calibration error .42 vs .38; best no-retrieval baseline .208 (GPT-4-1106-Preview). In a selective setting where the system abstains by heuristics, it outperformed the crowd. — [arXiv 2402.18563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563)
- [B, industry preprint, 10 November 2025] AIA Forecaster (Bridgewater AIA Labs): agentic search over high-quality news, a supervisor agent that reconciles disparate forecasts, and statistical calibration. Claims performance equal to human superforecasters on ForecastBench. On a harder benchmark built from liquid prediction markets it *underperforms market consensus*, but an ensemble of AIA Forecaster plus market consensus beats consensus alone, which the authors read as additive information; they note the sample is modest. — [arXiv 2511.07678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07678)
- [A] Lu, "Evaluating LLMs on Real-World Forecasting Against Expert Forecasters" (2025): 464 Metaculus questions; frontier models "ostensibly surpass the human crowd" but significantly underperform expert forecasters (o3 Brier 0.1352 vs expert *median* 0.0225). The paper's own table lists an expert *mean* of 0.1573 on the 157-question subset, so check which statistic is compared. — [arXiv 2507.04562](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04562)

#### Fine-tuned forecasters
- [A/B] Turtel et al., "Outcome-based Reinforcement Learning to Predict the Future" (Lightning Rod Labs; TMLR): fine-tuning on resolved outcomes; the ReMax ensemble matches or surpasses o1 on accuracy with better calibration. ### Inferences
- Across four independent set-ups, the standalone LLM loses to or ties the liquid-market price: Foresight-32B (0.199 vs 0.170), AIA Forecaster (underperforms consensus), Prophet Arena without market input (0.191 vs 0.187) and the TU Delft thesis. I found no peer-reviewed paper reporting a statistically significant out-of-sample Brier advantage of an LLM system over Polymarket or Kalshi prices.
- The recurring positive pattern is *complementarity*: LLM plus market beats market alone (AIA Forecaster), and human plus machine averaging beats either alone (Schoenegger). That supports LLMs as one input to a pricing model rather than as a replacement for the market price.
- Fine-tuning on outcomes improves calibration (lower ECE) more than it improves resolution; the simulated trading gains are small in absolute terms ($52 vs $39 on about 1,265 one-share bets) and exclude costs as far as the snippets show.
- Models named in the 2024–2025 papers (GPT-4, Claude 2, o1, o3, GPT-4.5) are two or more generations behind those in the 2026 benchmarks (GPT-5.1+, Grok 4.20, Gemini 3, Claude Opus 4.5–4.7), so old capability estimates are superseded, though the methodological lessons are not.

## 3. Methodological pitfalls: which positive results survive

### Takeaway
Backtests are the weakest evidence: date-filtered web search alone inflated one model's accuracy from Brier 0.242 to 0.108, so only live, forward-tested results deserve weight. Among live results, "parity with expert humans" survives scrutiny; every "beats the market" or "profitable" result found fails at least one of these checks: market price supplied as an input, short window, small sample, selection filters, missing costs, or self-reporting.

### Cited Findings
- [B] Search-engine leakage is large. An audit of Google's `before:` filter and DuckDuckGo's date-range filter over about 390 resolved forecasting questions found leakage "pervasive"; gpt-oss-120b scored Brier 0.108 with the leaky retrieved documents vs 0.242 with leak-free documents. Leak paths: articles updated after publication, related-content modules, unreliable date metadata and absence-based signals. The authors recommend frozen, time-stamped web snapshots. — [El Lahib et al., arXiv 2602.00758](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00758v1)
- [A] Leakage also enters through the retriever itself: retrieval models trained on later data carry future associations (a date-restricted search for "January 6th" returns documents with an unusually strong US-politics link for 2020), and document date metadata is often wrong; backtests also select questions at a past time T with knowledge of what happened later. — [Paleka et al., arXiv 2506.00723](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00723v1)
- [A/B] Live, forward-looking designs avoid leakage by construction: ForecastBench, Metaculus FutureEval, Prophet Arena, FutureX, Foresight Arena (commit–reveal on-chain), Prediction Arena (live trades). PolyBench uses timestamp-locked market snapshots; KalshiBench restricts evaluation to questions resolving after 1 October 2025; Halawi et al. used questions opened after the knowledge cutoff. — [Prophet Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638); [PolyBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14199); [KalshiBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16030); [Halawi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563)
- [B/C] Market price in the prompt, and stale-price comparisons: on ForecastBench market questions a forecaster is handed a market price; 442 of 527 scored worse than that price and none demonstrably beat it; several LLMs copy the price when it is in the prompt, and entrants may fetch fresher prices at submission time. — [Copier Line](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/8YuG9EuhyYqaNBt7F/the-copier-line-what-forecastbench-s-market-scores-actually); [Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.substack.com/p/what-forecastbench-doesnt-measure)
- [C] Stale human baseline: the ForecastBench superforecaster benchmark comes from a single 2024 engagement on a fixed question set, so the AI-vs-superforecaster comparison is extrapolated rather than head-to-head; FRI scheduled a fresh superforecaster round for October 2026. — [Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.substack.com/p/what-forecastbench-doesnt-measure); [FRI on X](https://x.com/Research_FRI/status/2102411966103093386)
- [A] Unequal post-processing: Metaculus's synthesis notes commentary that a widely cited "LLM beats the human crowd" result holds only after Platt-scaling the LLM output (not applied to the crowd), with possible data contamination as well. — [Metaculus, "AI Forecasting in 2026"](https://www.metaculus.com/notebooks/43363/ai-forecasting-in-2026/)
- [A] Mean-versus-median mixing: Lu's headline compares o3's score with the experts' *median* Brier (0.0225), while the experts' *mean* on the same subset is 0.1573; a 41-question hold-out is noted by the author as noisy. — [arXiv 2507.04562](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04562)
- [B/C] Selection filters drive live returns: in the 26-day Kalshi run, the 2–14 day horizon, ≥10¢ move and category exclusions determine the book and hence the +80.33% ROI; slippage is not bounded. — [Pith Review](https://pith.science/paper/2607.06166)
- [B] Venue and market selection change the sign of results: the same cohort of models averaged −22.6% on Kalshi and −1.1% on Polymarket. — [Prediction Arena, arXiv 2604.07355](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07355)
- [B] Costs and execution: Lightning Rod's profit figures are one-hypothetical-share simulations; Prophet Arena's return metric is an equal-budget simulated bet per event; PolyBench simulates order-book execution; "When do prophets profit" backtests against historical bid/ask and trades live with real spreads and fees; Prediction Arena executes real orders. — [Turtel et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17989v4); [Prophet Arena ranking blog](https://ai-prophet.github.io/pm_ranking/blogpost/ranking_llm_250727.html); [PolyBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14199); [arXiv 2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06166); [Prediction Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07355)
- [B/C] Short windows: Prediction Arena 57 days (and a 3-day paper-trading cohort); the proper-betting live run 26 days; PolyBench one week of market states (6–12 February 2026); BallotBench reported after 24 hours; KellyBench a single season (its authors say this "restricts us from making overly strong conclusions"); Bloomberg notes AI trading arenas "run for only short periods". — [Emergent Mind on Prediction Arena](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2604.07355); [Prophet Arena research page](https://www.prophetarena.co/research/when-do-prophets-profit); [PolyBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.14199); [BallotBench summary](https://digg.com/ai/hqtqjfb1); [KellyBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27865); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-06/ai-bots-auditioning-for-wall-street-trading-are-mostly-losing)
- [B/C/D] Small samples and many entrants: Apodex's claim rests on 70 questions; Grok's PolyBench gain on five bets; Schoenegger's study on 31 questions; Lightning Rod's p = 0.037 appears in one of several paper versions with different test sets and betting rules; Prophet Arena compares 23 models whose Brier scores sit in a narrow band; ForecastBench has 527 entrants. — [Apodex](https://x.com/Apodex_AI/status/2104987876165619862); [PolyBench write-up](https://levelup.gitconnected.com/seven-ai-models-traded-prediction-markets-for-a-week-five-lost-money-111f228c6e6c); [Schoenegger et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379); [Turtel et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17989v4); [Prophet Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638); [Copier Line](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/8YuG9EuhyYqaNBt7F/the-copier-line-what-forecastbench-s-market-scores-actually)
- [B] PnL is a confounded measure of forecasting skill: profit mixes accuracy with timing and risk choices (Foresight Arena); simple strategies can profit with no accuracy edge and accurate forecasters can lose under common sizing rules (When do prophets profit). — [Foresight Arena](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.00420); [arXiv 2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06166)
- [C] Tournament format effects: the Metaculus Cup rewards coverage and frequent updating, which favours tireless bots independent of per-question accuracy. — [explainx.ai](https://explainx.ai/blog/ai-beats-forecasters-metaculus-cup-coverage-2026)

## 4. Strengths and weaknesses by question type, and known biases

### Takeaway
LLM forecasters look comparatively strongest at longer horizons, on numeric questions, and where the crowd or market is itself uncertain; they are weakest close to resolution (where markets update fastest), on sports, and at turning forecasts into bet sizes. Documented biases are overconfidence (especially without retrieval, with extended reasoning, or when stating trade confidence), acquiescence (yes-bias), heavy reliance on any market price shown in the prompt, and failure to adapt after losses; evidence on niche long-tail markets, recency bias and cross-question consistency was not obtained in this session.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Question format (Metaculus Spring 2026, bots vs Pros): binary −2.51, multiple-choice −5.02, numeric +3.47, so bots were relatively better on numeric questions and worst on multiple-choice. — [Metaculus Spring 2026 analysis](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/wZBbDqzfBjYG58CxK/futureeval-spring-results-pros-beat-bots-but-the-gap-is)
- [A] Bots lose more often but win bigger: Pros outscored bots on 64% of questions, yet many bot wins were by larger margins. — [Metaculus Spring 2026 analysis](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/wZBbDqzfBjYG58CxK/futureeval-spring-results-pros-beat-bots-but-the-gap-is)
- [A] Horizon: on Prophet Arena some LLMs outperform the market baseline at longer horizons, while the market is most accurate near resolution because it reacts quickly to new signals. — [arXiv 2510.17638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638)
- [A] Domain: sports events are "intrinsically more challenging to predict" on Prophet Arena. — [arXiv 2510.17638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638)
- [B] Sports betting specifically: every model lost over an EPL season; a simple Dixon–Coles statistical model beat three-fifths of the LLM agents and a human quant made +5.1%. — [KellyBench, arXiv 2604.27865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27865)
- [A] Where the crowd is uncertain: Halawi et al.'s system beats the human crowd only in a selective setting chosen by heuristics. — [arXiv 2402.18563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563)
- [A] Calibration can be good with tools and context: all selected Prophet Arena LLMs were better calibrated than the Kalshi baseline (ECE ≤ 0.05 vs 0.069), and "Claude excels in calibration". — [arXiv 2510.17638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638); [papernotes summary](https://en.papernotes.org/ICLR2026/llm_evaluation/llm-as-a-prophet_understanding_predictive_intelligence_with_prophet_arena/)
- [B] Overconfidence without retrieval: on KalshiBench all five frontier models were overconfident (ECE 0.120 for Claude Opus 4.5 up to 0.395 for GPT-5.2-XHigh); four of five did worse than base rates; extended reasoning worsened calibration; models with similar accuracy differed 3× in calibration. — [arXiv 2512.16030](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16030)
- [C] Overconfidence when trading: PolyBench's five losing models reported average confidence 0.808–0.871. — [PolyBench write-up](https://levelup.gitconnected.com/seven-ai-models-traded-prediction-markets-for-a-week-five-lost-money-111f228c6e6c)
- [A] Recalibration helps: Platt scaling improved bot Brier scores in a May 2026 Metaculus analysis, i.e. raw bot probabilities are systematically miscalibrated in a correctable way. — [Metaculus, "AI Forecasting in 2026"](https://www.metaculus.com/notebooks/43363/ai-forecasting-in-2026/)
- [A] Acquiescence bias: the LLM crowd's mean forecast was significantly above 50% although outcomes split almost evenly. — [Schoenegger et al., arXiv 2402.19379](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379)
- [A/C] Anchoring on a supplied price or crowd forecast: GPT-4 and Claude 2 gained 17–28% accuracy when shown the human median; market data in context improved Prophet Arena Brier from 0.191 to 0.169; several ForecastBench LLMs copy the market forecast when it appears in the prompt. — [Schoenegger et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379); [Prophet Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638); [Good Judgment](https://goodjudgment.substack.com/p/what-forecastbench-doesnt-measure)
- [A] Diminishing value of crowd input for the best bots: Metaculus reports the community prediction "starts to provide little signal to the best bots". — [Metaculus, "AI Forecasting in 2026"](https://www.metaculus.com/notebooks/43363/ai-forecasting-in-2026/)
- [C] Failure to adapt: in KellyBench one model noticed its predicted 40% home-win rate was realising at 30% but never changed its code; the paper says models "fail to adapt strategies in response to failure". — [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/364406/ai-beat-sports-betting-market-top-8-models); [KellyBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27865)
- [A] Information-environment weaknesses: agents "can be fooled by fake web pages" and struggle with temporal validity (FutureX). — [arXiv 2508.11987](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11987)
- [A] Scaffolding matters more than the base model alone: top scaffolded bots beat their un-scaffolded baseline by 5–11 peer-score points per question (Fall 2025). — [Metaculus, "AI Forecasting in 2026"](https://www.metaculus.com/notebooks/43363/ai-forecasting-in-2026/)
- [A] Model "personalities" diverge: models often depart from market consensus in different directions, sometimes producing high simulated returns on single underdog bets (o3-mini's $1 → $9). — [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/336298/ai-now-matches-prediction-markets-in-forecasting-real-events-study-finds)

### Inferences
- "Hedging toward 50%" and "overconfidence" both appear, depending on set-up: models with retrieval and calibration layers are well calibrated or mildly under-confident (so Platt scaling helps), while models asked cold, or asked to express trade confidence, are overconfident. A trading system should not assume one direction of bias; it should measure it on its own pipeline.
- The horizon pattern points to the least-bad niche for an LLM forecaster: longer-dated questions where the market has not yet converged. That is also where capital is locked longest and where Polymarket/Kalshi prices show the largest calibration bias (KQ5), so any apparent edge there may be the known structural bias rather than model insight.
- Near resolution, the market's speed advantage dominates; an LLM polling every two hours is structurally late (news-reaction trading is covered by another researcher).
- Bots "lose often, win big" versus Pros: this is the profile of a forecaster that is sometimes confidently right where humans hedge, and also confidently wrong. In a market with spreads, the many small losses are paid in costs each time.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence was retrieved on LLM performance in niche, long-tail, thinly traded markets; this is a major gap, since that is where an informational edge is most plausible and where liquidity limits size.
- No session-verified evidence on recency bias or on logical inconsistency across related questions (see the unverified Paleka lead in KQ2).
- Text-heavy rule interpretation: a relevant paper was surfaced but not read ("Can LLMs Help Decentralized Dispute Arbitration? A Case Study of UMA-Resolved Markets on Polymarket", [arXiv 2604.15674](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.15674)).
- Base-rate-heavy questions: the only data point is indirect (KalshiBench: four of five models did worse than base rates when run without retrieval).
- Category-level Brier comparisons of LLM vs market (politics, economics, crypto, culture) were not in the snippets.

## 5. Market-efficiency baseline: how accurate and calibrated are Polymarket and Kalshi prices, and what is left after fees

### Takeaway
Polymarket and Kalshi prices are informative but not perfectly calibrated: several 2026 studies find a favourite–longshot / compression-toward-50% pattern (a 70¢ contract one month out resolves YES about 75% of the time), larger at longer horizons and varying by category. That is an edge of a few cents that a simple statistical rule can target, so it is thin after fees and spreads and is not evidence that LLM judgement is needed to capture it. (This section is under-researched: the search budget ran out during it.)

### Cited Findings
- [B, preprint, February 2026, revised August 2026] Le, "Decomposing Crowd Wisdom": 292 million trades across 327,000 contracts in v1, 353 million trades across 429,000 contracts in the revision, covering Kalshi and Polymarket. A contract trading at 70 cents one month out corresponds to a true probability closer to 75%, on both platforms. Political markets show the clearest distortion (prices compressed toward 50%), but the revision says the political effect is not robust on Polymarket. — [arXiv 2602.19520](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19520)
- [B, working paper, 2026] Bürgi, Deng and Whelan on Kalshi: prices become more accurate as markets approach closing but display a systematic favourite–longshot bias, which the authors attribute to modest belief disagreement plus a tendency to overstate small probabilities. — [GWU working paper 2026-001](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf); [CEPR VoxEU column](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/economics-kalshi-prediction-market)
- [B, senior thesis, April 2026] Princeton thesis on 188,509 resolved Polymarket markets: broad underconfidence consistent with favourite–longshot bias, present across all market domains and worse at longer time horizons. — [Princeton thesis record](https://theses-dissertations.princeton.edu/entities/publication/d5708372-5ea3-4252-b669-7f9cee07387c)
- [B, preprint, October 2026] "Time-Varying Favorite-Longshot Bias in Polymarket Weather Markets": the bias in daily temperature markets shifts over time; a single all-market calibration curve can hide differences across categories and horizons. — [arXiv 2610.13593](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.13593)
- [A] Market-price accuracy measured inside LLM benchmarks: Kalshi baseline Brier 0.187 and ECE 0.069 on more than 1,300 Prophet Arena events. — [arXiv 2510.17638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17638)
- [B] Polymarket price Brier 0.170 on 251 live questions (July–August 2025). — [Foresight-32B model card](https://huggingface.co/LightningRodLabs/foresight-32B/blob/main/README.md)
- [B] Market microstructure matters for who profits: on central-limit-order-book exchanges "informed forecasters routinely lose money while uninformed strategies can profit on simple heuristics". — [arXiv 2607.06166](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06166)
- [C] Who actually makes money (Bloomberg wallet analysis, 28 April 2026): more than 100,000 Polymarket accounts lost at least $1,000 since the start of 2025; a secondary summary says the top 1% of accounts, mostly bots, took more than 80% of profits. — [Bloomberg](https://bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-28/most-prediction-market-traders-are-losing-money-while-bots-rack-up-gains); [HackerNoon summary](https://hackernoon.com/why-ai-trading-agents-are-hard-to-trust)

### Inferences
- The calibration data define the size of the "free" statistical edge: about 5 percentage points at 70¢ one month out, i.e. roughly a 7% gross expected return on capital at risk for buying such favourites, before fees, spread and capital lock-up. Illustration only, using Kalshi's general taker-fee formula as I recall it (0.07 × P × (1 − P) per contract ≈ 1.5¢ at 70¢; **not verified in this session; check against the fees researcher's notes**): fee plus a 1–2¢ half-spread would consume roughly half to two-thirds of that 5¢.
- This bias is harvestable by a rule ("buy favourites, avoid or sell longshots, prefer longer horizons") with no LLM. Any LLM-agent backtest should therefore be benchmarked against that rule, not against zero.
- Prophet Arena's finding that LLMs are *better calibrated* than Kalshi (ECE ≤ 0.05 vs 0.069) while *not* beating it on Brier fits this picture: the market has a correctable calibration bias but superior resolution (it knows more about each event). LLMs fix the cheap part and lack the valuable part.
- Wallet-level evidence says profits are concentrated in automated accounts, but the snippets do not identify their strategies; market-making, cross-venue or structural arbitrage and latency strategies (covered by other researchers) are more likely explanations than LLM probability forecasting.
- Because calibration bias varies by category, horizon and time (weather-market study), a pooled calibration curve should not be applied mechanically to a specific market.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved (search budget exhausted): detailed calibration slopes by category and time-to-expiry in Le's paper; Bürgi–Deng–Whelan's returns by price bucket and maker/taker split; whether any study shows the favourite–longshot edge surviving fees; Polymarket's own accuracy statistics; cross-platform comparisons for the 2024 and 2026 elections; the full Bloomberg wallet analysis.
- Unverified lead: Bürgi, Deng and Whelan report that buyers of Kalshi contracts priced under 10¢ lose more than 60% of their stake on average, and that takers lose markedly more than makers (roughly −32% vs −10% average returns).
- Unverified lead: Clinton and Huang (Vanderbilt, 2025) studied the 2024 US election markets and reported day-level accuracy of about 93% for PredictIt, 78% for Kalshi and 67% for Polymarket, with persistent cross-exchange price gaps.
- Unverified lead: Reichenbach and Walther (SSRN, 2025) analysed Polymarket trade data and found prices broadly accurate, with a minority (around 30%) of traders profitable.
- Unverified lead: Polymarket's accuracy page, based on a Dune dashboard, has claimed roughly 90% accuracy one month before resolution and about 94% a few hours before; such figures are inflated by markets that are already near-certain.
- No source was found that estimates, in dollars, how much mispricing is available to a forecaster in thin long-tail markets after accounting for depth.

## 6. Real-world actors: funds, startups, AI-agent projects, and AI-lab / platform announcements

### Takeaway
Several organisations have verifiable *forecasting-benchmark* results (Mantic, Cassi AI, xAI, Google DeepMind, Bridgewater AIA Labs, Lightning Rod Labs, the Prophet Arena team), but I found none with an auditable, multi-month, real-money track record of beating Polymarket or Kalshi with LLM forecasts. ### Inferences
- The actors with the strongest verified forecasting results (Mantic, Cassi AI, Bridgewater AIA Labs) sell or use forecasts as an input to human or institutional decisions; none publishes prediction-market PnL.
