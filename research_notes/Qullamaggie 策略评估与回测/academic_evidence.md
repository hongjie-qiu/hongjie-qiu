## 1. Cross-sectional momentum: magnitude by era, performance since 2000/2009 and in 2020–2026, crashes, market-state dependence, where it concentrates

### Takeaway
Momentum is one of the best-replicated anomalies (Fama-French UMD: 0.62%/mo, t=4.6, 1927–Jul 2026; 0.89%/mo in 1964–1999), but in the value-weighted US universe it has earned nothing statistically reliable since 2000 (0.21%/mo, t=0.7) and is down 28% cumulatively from Jan 2009 to Jul 2026; calendar years since 2020 alternate in sign (+7, −2, +16, −24, +20, −2), and July 2026 was the 15th-worst month since 1927 (−12.3%). What survives sits mainly in small caps (and, in equal-weighted post-publication data, mostly on the loser leg rather than the winner leg), is negative after down markets, and crashes in rebounds.

#### Original and long-sample estimates

### Inferences
- "Leading stocks keep leading" is a real historical regularity, but its size in the investable value-weighted universe since 2000 (long leg +0.2–0.3%/mo over the market, t≈1) is an order of magnitude too small to explain large trading profits by itself.
- The long-short factor and long-only momentum indices can tell opposite stories in the same year (2025: UMD −2.3, MSCI USA Momentum +17.3%, equal to its parent), because the factor's result depends heavily on what the *losers* do. A long-only breakout trader is exposed to the winner leg plus market beta, not to UMD.
- Evidence that momentum is conditional on market state (positive after up markets, negative after down markets, catastrophic in bear-market rebounds) supports a rule of trading the long setup only in established uptrends and standing aside otherwise; it does not show that the transitions can be timed.
- The pockets where momentum has stayed measurable (small caps: 0.58%/mo, t=2.4, 2010–Jun 2026; high-turnover names; young/uncertain firms) are the pockets he trades — and also where trading costs and data-mining concerns are largest.
- 2020–2026 was a whipsaw regime: two of the fifteen worst UMD months in a century fall in Nov 2020 and Jan 2023, and a third in Jul 2026 after a +25% first half. The first half of 2026 (winner decile +57% in six months) is the kind of window in which a concentrated momentum trader could post extreme results; Feb 2021–Jul 2022 (winner decile −43%) is the opposite.

## 2. 52-week-high / new-high breakouts, volume, technical trading rules and opening-range breakouts

### Takeaway
Nearness to the 52-week high was a modest predictor in its original sample (0.45%/mo, t=2.0, 1963–2001) and has faded since publication (0.18%/mo, t=0.6, 2005–2023); no peer-reviewed study tests the chart-pattern breakout he trades, and the technical-rule literature is dominated by data-snooping failures. The only strongly positive "breakout" results (Zarattini et al.'s opening-range breakouts on "stocks in play": Sharpe 2.81 net of commissions, 2016–2023) are recent working papers on an intraday strategy with untested slippage and capacity.

### Cited Findings

#### 52-week high
- George & Hwang (2004), all CRSP stocks 1963–2001: long the 30% of stocks nearest their 52-week high, short the 30% furthest, equal-weighted, 6-month hold, earns about 0.45%/mo — the same order as JT and industry momentum in that sample ("all around 0.45% per month"); nearness to the high dominates past returns in forecasting regressions and the profits do not reverse in the long run. **[S]** — [JF 2004 record](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00695.x); [CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/1284/technical-trading/the-52-week-high-as-a-momentum-indicator-for-individual-stocks/). OSAP records the headline cell as 0.45%/mo with t=2.00 and notes that the sign of the effect flips between January and the other months. **[D]** — [OSAP SignalDoc.csv](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/SignalDoc.csv)
- Replication and decay: 0.47%/mo (t=1.89) in-sample; −0.29%/mo in 2002–2004; **0.18%/mo (t=0.56) post-publication 2005–2023**. Post-publication terciles (equal-weighted): near-high 0.61%/mo, middle 0.79, far-from-high 0.42 — the near-high group earned *less* than the middle group. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)
- Follow-ups, qualitative: Huddart, Lang & Yetman (2009) find volume spikes when a stock crosses its 52-week high or low and positive subsequent returns, larger for small stocks. **[M]** — [Management Science 2009](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1080.0920)

#### Volume
- Gervais, Kaniel & Mingelgrin (2001), NYSE 1963–1996: stocks with unusually high (low) volume over a day or week appreciate (depreciate) over the following month — the "high-volume return premium", attributed to a visibility shock. Direction only; magnitude not verified. **[M]** — [JF 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00349)
- High turnover as a level characteristic predicts *lower* returns in the original samples and nothing since: share turnover (Datar, Naik & Radcliffe 1998) 0.74%/mo in-sample → 0.07%/mo (t=0.34) post-publication; turnover volatility 0.80 → −0.01. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)

#### Technical trading rules
- Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (1992), Dow Jones Industrial Average 1897–1986, 26 moving-average and trading-range-break rules: days following buy signals returned ≈+12% annualised versus ≈−7% following sell signals. Index-level, before costs. **[M]** — [JF 1992](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04681.x)
- Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999): across 7,846 rules the BLL result survives a data-snooping correction in-sample, but the best rule shows no significant out-of-sample performance in 1987–1996. **[M]** — [JF 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00163)
- Bajgrowicz & Scaillet (2012), DJIA 1897–2011 with false-discovery-rate control: an investor could not have selected the future best rules ex ante, and profits vanish after transaction costs. **[M]** — [JFE 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2012.06.001)
- Park & Irwin (2007) survey: of 95 modern studies, 56 report positive results, 20 negative, 19 mixed — with the caveat that most positive studies are exposed to data snooping, ex-post rule selection and poor cost/risk estimation. **[M]** — [J. Economic Surveys 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6419.2007.00519.x)
- Han, Yang & Zhou (2013), 1963–2009: a 10-day moving-average timing rule applied to volatility-sorted decile *portfolios* beats buy-and-hold, with abnormal returns rising with volatility (roughly high-single-digit to high-teens per year from memory; uncertain). Portfolio-level, before costs. **[M]** — [JFQA 2013](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109013000586)
- Han, Zhou & Zhu (2016) "trend factor" built from moving averages of 3 to 1,000 days: 1.63%/mo (t=15.0) in 1930–2014 as published; replicated 1.71%/mo (t=13.4); **post-publication 2017–2023: 0.20%/mo (t=0.47)**, with the strongest-trend quintile earning 0.86%/mo against 0.66 for the weakest. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)

#### Trend-following on single stocks (not peer-reviewed)
- Wilcox & Crittenden (2005, Blackstar Funds white paper), ≈24,000 US securities including delisted, 1983–2004: buy at a new all-time high, exit on a 10-ATR trailing stop; about 18,000 trades, 49.3% winners, average win ≈51% versus average loss ≈−20% (ratio ≈2.56), expectancy ≈15% per trade. **[M]** — [paper copy](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/trend.pdf)
- Zarattini, Pagani & Wilcox (2025, working paper) update the same idea for 1950–2024 and report that fewer than 7% of trades generate the cumulative profit. From memory, uncertain; not opened. **[M]** — [Concretum papers page](https://concretumgroup.com/papers/)

#### Opening-range breakouts on "stocks in play"
- Zarattini, Barbon & Aziz (2024, SSRN / Swiss Finance Institute working paper): 5-minute opening-range breakout, more than 7,000 US stocks, Jan 2016–Dec 2023; restricting each day to the 20 stocks with the highest opening relative volume (price > $5, average volume ≥ 1M shares, ATR > $0.50, relative volume ≥ 100%), stop at 10% of ATR, exit at the close: total net return above 1,600%, **41.6% annualised, Sharpe 2.81 (S&P 500: ≈15%, 0.78), annualised alpha 36%**, stated net of commissions. **[S]** (summaries; paper not opened) — [Concretum](https://concretumgroup.com/a-profitable-day-trading-strategy-for-the-u-s-equity-market/); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/chf/rpseri/rp2498.html); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=45379)
- Independent re-implementations on QuantConnect report Sharpe ratios of about 2.2–2.4 and a market beta near zero. **[S]** — [QuantConnect research post](https://www.quantconnect.com/research/18444/opening-range-breakout-for-stocks-in-play/)
- Lou, Polk & Skouras (2019): for US stocks the abnormal returns to momentum strategies are earned entirely overnight, with intraday returns of the opposite sign. **[M]** — [JFE 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2019.03.011)

### Inferences
- George & Hwang test *being near* the high with monthly rebalancing and a 6-month hold, not the event of breaking out; their effect is half the size of JT momentum and has not survived publication in equal-weighted data. It lends plausibility to anchoring at prior highs, not to an edge at the breakout bar.
- The closest tests of "buy the new high, trail a stop" are practitioner papers. They show the same qualitative payoff shape as the one in the brief — fewer than half of trades win and a small minority of trades produce the profit — though with a higher hit rate (about 49%) than the 25–30% in the brief; they are not peer-reviewed, and Wilcox & Crittenden predates most of the post-2000 decay.
- The opening-range-breakout papers support the narrow claim that stocks with abnormal opening volume tend to continue intraday in the direction of the first break. They do not test multi-day holding, and Lou, Polk & Skouras's overnight/intraday split is a reminder that intraday entry and overnight holding harvest different return components.
- The decay of the moving-average "trend factor" from 1.63%/mo to 0.20%/mo after publication is a cautionary data point for any rule built on 10/20/50-day averages.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests flag / tight-consolidation breakouts with the filters commonly attributed to him (a large prior run, high average daily range, entry on the opening-range high, stop at the day's low — my understanding of the setup, not sourced in this note). I am not aware of one.
- Magnitude of the Gervais–Kaniel–Mingelgrin premium, and the numbers in Zarattini & Aziz (2023, QQQ opening-range breakout) and Zarattini, Pagani & Wilcox (2025), could not be verified.
- The commission and slippage assumptions of Zarattini, Barbon & Aziz were not visible in search results (from memory the commission is $0.0035/share and no slippage is modelled — unverified); win rate and capacity are unknown to me.

## 3. Earnings gaps and post-earnings-announcement drift (the "episodic pivot")

### Takeaway
Drift after earnings news is the best-supported mechanism of the three: sorting on the announcement-window return itself earned 1.15%/mo in 1977–1992 and still 0.85%/mo (t=7.5) in 1997–2023 in equal-weighted data, and price jumps accompanied by fundamental information drift while no-news jumps reverse. But the effect is a few percent per quarter on diversified portfolios, is concentrated in small and illiquid stocks where costs absorb most of it, and has been reported as gone for non-microcaps since 2006.

### Cited Findings

#### Size of the drift in the classic samples
- Ball & Brown (1968) first documented that returns keep moving in the direction of the earnings surprise after the announcement. **[M]** — [J. Accounting Research 1968](https://doi.org/10.2307/2490232)
- Foster, Olsen & Shevlin (1984): standardized-unexpected-earnings (SUE) decile L-S, replicated at 1.13%/mo (t=4.85) for 1974–1981. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)
- Bernard & Thomas (1989), NYSE/AMEX 1974–1986: long the top SUE decile, short the bottom, earns ≈4.2% over the 60 trading days after the announcement (≈18% annualised); the drift is larger for small firms (≈5% small, ≈4.5% medium, ≈2.8% large). **[M]** — [J. Accounting Research 1989](https://doi.org/10.2307/2491062)
- Bernard & Thomas (1990): a disproportionate part of the drift arrives in the 3-day windows around the *next* announcements (≈+1.3% at the next quarter, ≈+0.7% the quarter after, then a partial reversal at the fourth), as if prices ignore the autocorrelation of earnings surprises. **[M]** — [J. Accounting & Economics 1990](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-4101(90)90008-R)
- Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok (1996), 1977–1993: six-month L-S spreads of ≈8.8% for past return, ≈6.8% for SUE, ≈5.9% for the announcement abnormal return and ≈7.7% for analyst revisions; each has incremental power. **[M]** — [JF 1996](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1996.tb05222.x). OSAP records their regression t-statistics as 9.25 (announcement return) and 4.07 (revisions). **[D]** — [OSAP SignalDoc.csv](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/SignalDoc.csv)
- Brandt, Kishore, Santa-Clara & Venkatachalam (2008, working paper): sorting on the 3-day earnings-announcement return (EAR) earns an abnormal 7.55% per year, 1.3 points more than SUE; using both gives about 12.5% per year; unlike SUE, the EAR effect does not reverse after three quarters. **[S]** (Quantpedia summary; sample period not confirmed) — [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/post-earnings-announcement-effect/)

#### The "gap" signal after publication (OSAP, equal-weighted, gross, to Dec 2023)
- Earnings-announcement return (4-day market-adjusted return around the announcement, quintile L-S, monthly rebalanced): 1.15%/mo (t=12.75) in 1977–1992; 1.29%/mo in 1993–1996; **0.85%/mo (t=7.48) in 1997–2023**. Post-publication quintile returns: 0.53 / 0.83 / 0.97 / 1.06 / **1.38**%/mo — the top quintile beats the middle one by 0.41%/mo (≈5% per year) and the bottom lags it by 0.44%/mo. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)
- SUE post-publication 1985–2023: 0.39%/mo (t=4.67), down from 1.13; top decile 1.26%/mo versus ≈1.04–1.17 for the middle deciles and 0.87 for the bottom. Revenue surprise 0.73 → 0.36 (t=2.55); earnings-surprise streaks 1.09 → 0.66 (t=3.96); six-month analyst revisions 1.13 → 0.57 (t=3.24). **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)
- Daniel, Hirshleifer & Sun (2020) build a "PEAD" factor from the 4-day announcement return that earns roughly 0.6–0.65%/mo with a t-statistic above 7 in 1972–2014 (from memory, approximate). **[M]** — [RFS 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhz069)

#### Evidence that the drift has weakened or disappeared
- Martineau (2022, Critical Finance Review): prices now reflect earnings surprises on the announcement date; the drift began disappearing for non-microcap stocks in 2001 and was gone by 2006; it lingered in microcaps and has faded there recently. **[S]** (abstract and draft-version summaries) — [Critical Finance Review](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0122); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:now:jnlcfr:104.00000122)
- A UCLA Anderson Review brief ("Is Post-Earnings Announcement Drift a Thing Again?") reports work by Subrahmanyam (2025) in which the drift has a t-statistic of 2.18 with all stocks but 1.43 once microcaps are excluded. **[S]** (search summary only; the underlying paper was not opened) — [UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/is-post-earnings-announcement-drift-a-thing-again)
- Chordia, Goyal, Sadka, Sadka & Shivakumar (2009): the drift strategy earns ≈0.04%/mo in the most liquid stocks and ≈2.43%/mo in the least liquid, and transaction costs consume 70–100% of the paper profit there. **[M]** — [FAJ 2009](https://doi.org/10.2469/faj.v65.n4.3)
- Chordia, Subrahmanyam & Tong (2014): anomaly returns (including earnings and price momentum) roughly halved after decimalisation in 2001 as liquidity and arbitrage activity rose. **[M]** — [J. Accounting & Economics 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacceco.2014.06.001)

#### What makes the drift bigger or turns it into reversal
- Savor (2012, JFE): large price moves accompanied by analyst reports (information) are followed by drift; large moves with no information are followed by reversal; the drift appears only when the price move and the recommendation change have the same sign. **[S]** (abstract level; magnitudes not seen) — [JFE 2012 record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v106y2012i3p635-659.html)
- Govindaraj, Livnat, Savor & Zhao, "Large Price Changes and Subsequent Returns": a L-S portfolio built on information-backed large price changes earns 1.31% abnormal per month against size/value/momentum-matched firms. **[S]** (Alpha Architect summary) — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/follow-the-trend-and-the-sell-side/)
- Chan (2003): stocks with news headlines drift (especially after bad news); extreme movers with no news reverse the next month. **[M]** — [JFE 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-405X(03)00146-6)
- Inattention: with many same-day announcements the immediate reaction is weaker and the drift stronger (Hirshleifer, Lim & Teoh 2009); Friday announcements show a 15% lower immediate response and 70% higher delayed response (DellaVigna & Pollet 2009). **[M]** — [JF 2009a](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01501.x); [JF 2009b](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01447.x)
- Disposition effect: Frazzini (2006) finds the drift is strongest when holders' unrealised gains have the same sign as the news; an event strategy conditioned on this earns monthly alphas above 200 basis points. **[M]** — [JF 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2006.00896.x)
- High-turnover one-month winners continue rather than reverse (+16.4% per year in the top turnover decile). **[S]** — [Alpha Architect on Medhat & Schmeling](https://alphaarchitect.com/short-term-momentum/)
- Lead-lag "sympathy" effects: small stocks follow the prior-month return of the big stocks in their industry (Hou 2007) — 2.32%/mo (t=9.5) in 1972–2001, **0.61%/mo (t=2.31) in 2008–2023**. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)

### Inferences
- The academic object closest to an episodic pivot is the announcement-window return (EAR/ABR), i.e. the gap itself. It is the rare signal whose L-S return kept three-quarters of its size and a t-statistic of 7.5 for 27 years after publication — but in an equal-weighted design that over-represents small and micro caps, gross of costs.
- The drift in those studies is measured from *after* the announcement window, over one to six months, and averages a few percent per quarter for a diversified quintile. A trader buying the open of the gap day is exposed first to the unresolved part of the announcement-day move, which these studies do not isolate.
- The filters commonly attributed to the setup (my understanding, not sourced in this note) line up with documented amplifiers: a fundamental catalyst (Savor's "information"), heavy volume (high-turnover continuation), prior neglect (inattention), and small/mid-cap size. The same literature says the liquid large caps, where large size can be deployed, show little or no drift since the mid-2000s.
- Gap-ups without a fundamental reason belong to the "no-information → reversal" branch; the same chart pattern has opposite expected drift depending on the cause.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that conditions specifically on large (≥10%) earnings-day gap-ups with intraday entry, or that reports the distribution (win rate, right tail) of single-stock outcomes after such gaps.
- Papers I believe are relevant but could not verify numerically: Jiang & Zhu (2017, JFE) on jumps and short-term underreaction; Zhou & Zhu (2012, FAJ) on jumps around earnings; Pritamani & Singal (2001); Garfinkel & Sokobin (2006) on announcement volume; George, Hwang & Li on the 52-week high and drift; Milian (2015) on reversal of announcement returns in heavily arbitraged, optioned stocks.
- The exact microcap timing in Martineau's published version, and the content of the 2025 Subrahmanyam paper, were seen only through search summaries.

## 4. Stops, low win rates and right tails

### Takeaway
A low hit rate with a few very large winners is what the cross-section of stock returns looks like: most stocks lose to T-bills over their lives and about 4% account for all net wealth creation. Stop-losses reduce expected return under a random walk and help only when returns trend; applied to momentum portfolios, a 10% stop historically cut the worst month from −49.8% to −11.3% and raised mean return, before costs.

### Cited Findings
- Bessembinder (2018, JFE), CRSP common stocks since 1926: the majority (about 58%) have lifetime buy-and-hold returns below one-month Treasuries; the best-performing 4% of companies (1,092 of nearly 26,000) account for the entire net gain of the market over T-bills; the most frequent lifetime outcome is −100%. **[S]** — [ASU summary of the paper](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/department-finance/faculty-research/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills); [ASU news release](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/treasury-bills-outperform-stocks-say/). Same paper: only about 47.8% of individual monthly stock returns exceed the T-bill rate. **[M]** — [JFE 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2018.06.004)
- Global extension (1990/91–2020): 55.2% of US stocks and 57.4% of non-US stocks underperform one-month T-bills. **[S]** (search summary) — [ASU paper page](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/department-finance/faculty-research/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)
- Kaminski & Lo (2014, J. Financial Markets): under a random walk the "stopping premium" is always negative; with positive serial correlation (momentum) it can be positive. Empirically, in the working-paper version a stop-loss rule switching from US equities into long-term bonds (monthly data 1950–2004) added 50–100 basis points per month during stop-out periods; the published version uses daily futures data 1993–2011 and finds positive stopping premia at longer sampling frequencies. **[S]** — [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114876); [journal record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:234-254)
- Han, Zhou & Zhu (working paper, 2014/2016), US stocks 1926–2013: adding a 10% stop to each stock in an equal-weighted momentum portfolio cuts the worst monthly loss from −49.79% to −11.34%, raises the mean from 1.01% to 1.73%/mo, lowers the standard deviation from 6.07% to 4.67%, and more than doubles the Sharpe ratio (0.165 → 0.369); value-weighted worst month −65.34% → −23.69%. Gross of the extra trading. **[S]** — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/stop-losses-to-avoid-stock-momentum-crashes/)
- Alpha Architect's commentary on that paper suggests haircutting the result by 300–600 basis points for costs and frictions. **[S]** — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/)
- Lo & Remorov (2017): for individual US stocks, tight stop-loss rules underperform buy-and-hold once transaction costs are included unless serial correlation is strong. Qualitative. **[M]** — [J. Financial Markets 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2017.02.003)
- Odean (1998): individual investors realise gains about 1.5 times as readily as losses, and the winners they sell outperform the losers they keep by 3.4 percentage points over the next year — the opposite of "cut losers, ride winners". **[M]** — [JF 1998](https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00072)
- Practitioner evidence on the payoff shape of single-stock trend-following: 49.3% winners with a 2.56 win/loss ratio (Wilcox & Crittenden). **[M]** — [paper copy](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/trend.pdf)
- Portfolio-level risk management of momentum (volatility scaling) nearly doubles the Sharpe ratio, 0.53 → 0.97. **[S]** — [CXO Advisory on Barroso & Santa-Clara](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/avoiding-momentum-strategy-crashes/)

### Inferences
- A 25–30% win rate is not, by itself, a red flag: with a positively skewed cross-section and a stop that truncates the left tail, a trend-following book should lose on most trades and depend on a few outliers.
- The stop-loss literature gives conditional support: stops help *if* the traded stocks trend. For momentum stocks historically they did, at the portfolio level, before costs. The literature does not test tight intraday stops (low of day, 1× average daily range), which trigger far more often than a 10% monthly stop and convert spread and slippage into the dominant cost.
- The disposition-effect evidence implies that most retail traders do the reverse of his exit rules, which is one reason the documented drift exists.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence found on moving-average trailing exits (10/20-day) on single stocks, or on partial profit-taking after 3–5 days.
- Arithmetic implied by a low win rate (my calculation, not from a source): at a 25% win rate an expectancy of 0.2–0.5R per trade needs an average win of 3.8–5.0R; at 30% it needs 3.0–4.0R. Doubling an account in a year needs roughly 70R net at 1% risk per trade, or roughly 140R at 0.5%.

## 5. Parabolic shorts: reversal, lottery stocks, attention spikes and the cost of being short

### Takeaway
Extreme recent winners and "lottery" stocks do earn low subsequent returns on average (prior-month winner decile ≈0.4%/mo versus ≈1.1%/mo for the median stock since 1991; highest-MAX decile ≈1%/mo below the lowest in 1962–2005; Robinhood herding stocks −4.7% over 20 days). But the overpricing is concentrated in stocks that are expensive or impossible to borrow — the shorting premium falls from 1.43%/mo gross to 0.91% net and anomalies vanish in the 80% of stocks with low fees — and run-ups raise crash probability without reliably predicting low average returns or the timing of the top.

### Cited Findings

#### Short-term reversal
- Jegadeesh (1990), 1934–1987: the spread between extreme deciles sorted on last month's return is 2.49%/mo (1.99%/mo, t=12.4, on the CAPM-alpha basis recorded by OSAP). **[M]** for 2.49, **[D]** for 1.99 — [JF 1990](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1990.tb05110.x); [OSAP SignalDoc.csv](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/SignalDoc.csv)
- Lehmann (1990): last week's winners return about −0.35% to −0.55% the next week and last week's losers +0.86% to +1.24%; the zero-cost contrarian portfolio profits in roughly 90% of weeks, before costs. **[M]** — [QJE 1990](https://doi.org/10.2307/2937816)
- Replication and decay (equal-weighted deciles, gross): 2.97%/mo (t=14.2) in-sample; 1.63%/mo (t=4.13) in 1991–2023. Post-publication decile returns: last month's top winners **0.41%/mo**, middle deciles ≈1.07–1.14, last month's worst losers 2.04. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)
- Avramov, Chordia & Goyal (2006): reversal profits sit in high-turnover, illiquid stocks and are smaller than plausible transaction costs. **[M]** — [JF 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2006.01060.x)
- Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz put the break-even capacity of short-term reversal at $9 billion in the US, far below momentum's $52 billion. **[M]** — [SSRN 2294498](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2294498)

#### MAX / lottery effect
- Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw (2011), 1962–2005: raw and risk-adjusted return differences between the lowest and highest deciles of maximum daily return in the prior month exceed 1%/mo. **[S]** (abstract) — [JFE 2011 record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:99:y:2011:i:2:p:427-446). OSAP records the value-weighted decile spread as 1.03%/mo (t=2.83). **[D]** — [OSAP SignalDoc.csv](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/SignalDoc.csv)
- Replication and decay (value-weighted): 0.89%/mo (t=2.74) in 1962–2005; **0.34%/mo (t=0.64) in 2012–2023**. The relation is non-linear: post-publication the highest-MAX decile earned 0.30%/mo against ≈1.0–1.25 for the middle deciles, but the lowest-MAX decile earned only 0.63. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)
- A 2022 Critical Finance Review paper argues the post-high-MAX underperformance is a general overreaction phenomenon, not confined to stocks identifiable ex ante as lottery-like. **[S]** — [Critical Finance Review](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0123)
- The related volatility signals show the same decay: idiosyncratic volatility 0.97 → 0.33%/mo (t=0.83); none of OSAP's six volatility-family signals keeps t>1.96 post-publication. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)

#### Attention-driven spikes and run-ups
- Barber & Odean (2008): individual investors are net buyers of attention-grabbing stocks — those in the news, with abnormal volume, or with extreme one-day returns. **[M]** — [RFS 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm079)
- Da, Engelberg & Gao (2011): a rise in Google search volume predicts higher prices over the next two weeks and a reversal within the year. **[M]** — [JF 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2011.01679.x)
- Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz (2022): the top stocks bought by Robinhood users each day have average 20-day abnormal returns of −4.7%. **[M]** — [JF 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13183)
- Greenwood, Shleifer & You (2019), US industries 1926–2014: a 100% run-up over two years does *not* on average predict unusually low subsequent returns, but it raises the probability of a crash sharply (about half of 40 such episodes crashed; about 80% after a 150% run-up); characteristics of the run-up (volatility, issuance, turnover acceleration, young firms) help forecast which ones crash; prices typically rise further before peaking. **[M]** — [JFE 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2018.09.002)
- Brunnermeier & Nagel (2004): hedge funds rode the technology bubble rather than shorting it, and cut exposure stock by stock before prices fell. **[M]** — [JF 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00690.x)
- Information matters for direction: large price moves without news reverse; those with fundamental information drift. **[S]** — [Savor 2012 record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v106y2012i3p635-659.html)

#### Borrow cost, constraints and squeeze risk
- D'Avolio (2002), Apr 2000–Sep 2001: 91% of stocks lent are "general collateral" with fees under 1% per year (value-weighted average 0.25%); 9% are "special" with a mean fee of 4.3%; fewer than 1% carry fees above 10%; about 2% of stocks on loan are recalled in an average month; specialness is more likely for small, low-institutional-ownership, high-turnover, glamour and IPO stocks. **[M]** — [JFE 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-405X(02)00206-4)
- Drechsler & Drechsler (2014, NBER), Jan 2004–2012/13: the cheap-minus-expensive-to-short portfolio earns **1.43%/mo gross, 0.91%/mo net of fees**, with a 1.53% four-factor alpha; eight well-known anomalies "effectively disappear" in the 80% of stocks with low fees and are greatly amplified among high-fee stocks. **[S]** (abstract) — [NBER w20282](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20282); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/short-selling/shorting-fee-as-a-stock-return-predictor/)
- Muravyev, Pearson & Pollet ("Anomalies and Their Short-Sale Costs"): using option-implied borrow fees, the minority of high-fee stock-days accounts for most anomaly L-S profit, and net of fees the short legs earn roughly nothing; in low-fee stocks anomaly returns are small. From memory, approximate; link ID from memory. **[M]** — [SSRN 4266059](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4266059)
- Engelberg, Reed & Ringgenberg (2018): short-selling *risk* — uncertainty about future fees and recalls — is itself priced; stocks with more of it have lower future returns and less short selling. **[M]** — [JF 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12601)
- Short sellers do profit on average, gross: heavily shorted NYSE stocks underperform lightly shorted ones by 1.16%/mo, 2000–2004 (Boehmer, Jones & Zhang 2008); short sellers increase shorting after short-term price rises and a L-S strategy on daily shorting flow earns about 1.4%/mo in 2005 (Diether, Lee & Werner 2009). **[M]** — [JF 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01324.x); [RFS 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhn047)
- Short interest as a signal has not decayed: 0.82%/mo (t=5.3) in 1976–1993 and 0.93%/mo (t=3.7) in 2002–2023, equal-weighted quintiles, gross of borrow fees. **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)
- Squeeze illustration: GameStop closed at $17.25 on 4 Jan 2021 and $347.51 on 27 Jan 2021 (intraday high $483 on 28 Jan), with short interest above 100% of the float. From memory. **[M]** — [SEC staff report, Oct 2021](https://www.sec.gov/files/staff-report-equity-options-market-struction-conditions-early-2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- The direction of the parabolic-short thesis is supported: on average, the most extreme recent winners, highest-MAX stocks and attention-herding stocks underperform by roughly 0.5–1.5%/mo gross over the following month.
- Three things separate that average from a tradable edge. First, the underperformance lives where borrow is costly, unstable or unavailable; net-of-fee estimates are much smaller (0.91 vs 1.43) or near zero. Second, the payoff is negatively skewed for the short seller: a few squeezes dominate the loss distribution, the mirror image of Section 4. Third, the literature predicts *that* extreme run-ups tend to end badly, not *when*; Greenwood–Shleifer–You and Brunnermeier–Nagel both show informed capital preferring to wait for the turn.
- The entry rule commonly attributed to him (short the first failure after a multi-day vertical move, cover into the 10/20-day average — my understanding, not sourced in this note) is an attempt to solve the timing problem with price action. Nothing I found tests that rule; Diether–Lee–Werner's finding that short sellers who lean against short-term run-ups earn abnormal returns is the nearest supportive evidence, from a single year.
- "Information versus no information" cuts both ways: a vertical move on real news is in the drift branch, where the short has negative expectancy.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed estimate found of returns to shorting multi-day, multi-hundred-percent small-cap run-ups net of actual borrow fees, locates and buy-ins. Borrow fees on such names are often far above D'Avolio's averages; I have no citable distribution.
- Could not verify numbers for Kumar, Ruenzi & Ungeheuer ("Daily Winners and Losers"; I recall ≈1.6%/mo underperformance of stocks appearing on daily winner/loser lists), Asquith, Pathak & Ritter (2005; I recall −215 basis points/mo equal-weighted for short-constrained stocks, insignificant value-weighted), pump-and-dump studies, or academic work on short squeezes.
- The 2.49%/mo Jegadeesh figure, the D'Avolio fee distribution and the GameStop prices are from memory.

## 6. Implementation: trading costs, capacity, and what survives for small versus large accounts

### Takeaway
Estimates of what momentum keeps after costs range from "most of it, with $50 billion of capacity" (live institutional trades) to "roughly zero" (mutual-fund outcomes and post-publication net returns); all refer to diversified, patiently traded portfolios. Capacity is not a constraint for a small retail account, but the literature contains no measurement of the costs that matter for his style — market and stop orders into fast, gapping small/mid caps, and borrow on parabolic names.

### Cited Findings
- Lesmond, Schill & Zhou (2004): the stocks that generate momentum profits are disproportionately small, low-priced, high-beta and off-NYSE, with round-trip costs large enough that the profits are "illusory". Qualitative. **[M]** — [JFE 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-405X(03)00206-X)
- Korajczyk & Sadka (2004): with price impact, equal-weighted momentum breaks down quickly, but value- and liquidity-weighted winner portfolios stay profitable until about $4.5–5 billion (1999 dollars) is run by a single manager. **[M]** — [JF 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00656.x)
- Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016): for mid-turnover strategies such as momentum, effective-spread costs run about 20–57 basis points per month; momentum's spread falls from roughly 1.3%/mo gross to roughly 0.7%/mo net with capacity around $5 billion; strategies with one-sided turnover above 50% per month (e.g. short-term reversal) rarely survive; a buy/hold band is the most effective mitigation. Numbers approximate. **[M]** — [RFS 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhv063)
- Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz (2012/2018), about $1–1.7 trillion of live AQR trades from 1998: realised costs are a fraction of earlier estimates; break-even sizes in US equities are $103 billion (size), $83 billion (value), **$52 billion (momentum)** and $9 billion (short-term reversal). **[M]** — [SSRN 2294498](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2294498); [SSRN 3229719](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3229719)
- Patton & Weller (2020): comparing mutual-fund returns with paper factors, typical funds earn low after-cost returns to value and essentially none to momentum. Qualitative. **[M]** — [JFE 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2019.09.011)
- Chen & Velikov (2023): across 204 anomalies, after effective spreads and post-publication decay in the post-2005 era, the average net return is about 4 basis points per month and the strongest net at most about 10. **[M]** — [JFQA 2023](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109022000874)
- Earnings drift specifically: costs consume 70–100% of paper profits in the illiquid stocks where the drift is found. **[M]** — [FAJ 2009](https://doi.org/10.2469/faj.v65.n4.3)
- Stop-loss overlays add turnover; one practitioner haircut is 300–600 basis points per year. **[S]** — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/)
- Short side: fees take 0.52%/mo (1.43 → 0.91) off the shorting premium on average, and far more in the highest-fee names. **[S]** — [NBER w20282](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20282)
- Gross paper returns to the relevant signals after publication, for scale: JT momentum 0.80%/mo, 52-week high 0.18, announcement return 0.85, SUE 0.39, short-term reversal 1.63, MAX 0.34 (all L-S, mostly equal-weighted). **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)

### Inferences
- For a small account in liquid names, commissions at the main US retail brokers are zero and quoted spreads are narrow (general knowledge, not sourced here), so the institutional cost studies overstate the drag from *spread and impact*. What they do not capture is slippage on breakout and stop orders in the first minutes of trading and on gap days, which scales with volatility, not with account size.
- For a large account (tens of millions of dollars) concentrated in small/mid caps, the constraint reverses: a concentrated position can be a meaningful fraction of a day's volume. The capacity numbers above ($5–52 billion) are for diversified portfolios traded over days and say nothing about a concentrated book that must exit on a stop.
- The honest reading of the cost literature is that the *systematic* part of these anomalies leaves little after costs at scale. Any large net edge has to come from selection and timing within the anomaly, which the literature does not measure.

### Gaps
- No verified study of retail execution quality on volatile small caps at the open; I recall Schwarz, Barber, Huang, Jorion & Odean ("The 'Actual Retail Price' of Equity Trades") finding round-trip costs that vary several-fold across brokers, but could not verify figures.
- No citable distribution of borrow fees for parabolic small caps.
- All Section 6 paper numbers are from memory except those tagged [S] or [D].

## 7. Trader-level evidence: who actually makes money

### Takeaway
The base rate for retail short-horizon traders is strongly negative, but a small, persistent skilled tail exists: in Taiwan fewer than 1% of day traders are predictably profitable net of fees, while the top 500 earn about 38 basis points per day net; in Brazil 97% of those who persisted 300+ days lost money. I found no peer-reviewed study of discretionary swing/momentum retail traders or of celebrated individual traders, so his record cannot be benchmarked against a studied population.

### Cited Findings
- Barber & Odean (2000), 66,465 US discount-brokerage households, 1991–1996: the average household earned 16.4% per year net versus 17.9% for the market; the most active quintile earned 11.4%; average turnover was 75% a year. **[M]** — [JF 2000](https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00226)
- Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean (2009), all trades in Taiwan 1995–1999: individuals' aggregate portfolio loses 3.8 percentage points a year to trading, equal to 2.2% of GDP; nearly all of it comes from aggressive orders. **[M]** — [RFS 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhn046)
- Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean (2014, J. Financial Markets), Taiwan day traders 1992–2006: large, persistent cross-sectional differences in skill; the spread between top- and bottom-ranked traders exceeds 60 basis points per day. **[S]** (publication details and the "more than 60 basis points per day" fragment confirmed) — [journal record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:18:y:2014:i:c:p:1-24); [eScholarship copy](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/7k75v0qx). Same paper: the 500 top-ranked traders go on to earn about 61 basis points per day gross and 38 net; bottom-ranked traders −12 gross and −29 net; fewer than 1% of day traders are predictably profitable after fees. **[M]** — [J. Financial Markets 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.finmar.2013.05.006)
- Barber, Lee, Liu, Odean & Zhang (2020), same data: unprofitable day traders persist; about 74% of day-trading volume comes from traders with a history of losses and about 97% of day traders are likely to lose money. **[M]** — [RAPS 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/rapstu/raz006)
- Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti (2020), Brazil equity-index futures: of 19,646 individuals who began day trading in 2013–2015, 1,551 (7.9%) persisted for more than 300 sessions; **97% of those lost money, 1.1% earned more than the minimum wage and 0.5% more than a bank teller's starting salary**. **[S]** — [SSRN 3423101](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/investing-expertise/day-trading-a-bust/)
- Jordan & Diltz (2003), 324 US day traders 1998–1999: about twice as many lose as win, and roughly 20% are more than marginally profitable. **[M]** — [FAJ 2003](https://doi.org/10.2469/faj.v59.n6.2578)
- Coval, Hirshleifer & Shumway (2021; working paper 2005), US discount-brokerage accounts: performance persists — the top decile of traders by past record earns risk-adjusted returns of roughly 12–15 basis points per day on subsequent trades. **[M]** — [SSRN 364000](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=364000)
- Professionals as a base rate for skill versus luck: Barras, Scaillet & Wermers (2010) classify 75.4% of US equity mutual funds as zero-alpha, 24.0% as unskilled and 0.6% as skilled. **[M]** — [JF 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01527.x)
- Momentum as a style among professionals: Grinblatt, Titman & Wermers (1995) find 77% of mutual funds were momentum buyers and that these funds performed better. **[M]** — [AER 1995](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2950976)
- Attention-chasing retail flow loses: −4.7% abnormal over 20 days for top Robinhood purchases. **[M]** — [JF 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13183)
- Regulator tallies outside the US (derivatives, context only): India's SEBI reported 89% of individual equity-derivatives traders lost money in FY2022 and 93% over FY2022–24. From memory; link from memory. **[M]** — [SEBI study, Jan 2023](https://www.sebi.gov.in/reports-and-statistics/research/jan-2023/study-analysis-of-profit-and-loss-of-individual-traders-dealing-in-equity-fando-segment_67525.html)

### Inferences
- The literature says two things at once: almost everyone loses, and the few who win tend to keep winning. A real, persistent top-0.1–1% tail is consistent with a genuinely skilled discretionary trader existing; it is also the population from which celebrated traders are selected after the fact.
- The studied samples are day traders and general retail accounts. Multi-day momentum swing trading with stops is not separately measured anywhere I could find, and retail investors as a group are contrarian rather than momentum traders, so the base rates above are an imperfect proxy.
- Persistence evidence is at horizons of days to a year and in basis points per day. Thirty-eight basis points per day net (the Taiwan top 500) compounds to roughly 150% a year on the capital actually deployed intraday — the only place in this literature where triple-digit annualised figures appear, and for the top 0.1% of several hundred thousand traders.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or audited academic evidence on Kullamägi, on US Investing Championship entrants, or on survivorship among "market wizard"-type traders was found. His track record is covered in the companion note `track_record_verification.md`, not here.
- Numbers for Barber et al. (2014, 2020), Coval et al., Jordan & Diltz and SEBI are from memory.

## 8. Synthesis: what the evidence does and does not support for each setup

### Takeaway
Each setup points at a real, documented anomaly, but the documented effects are of the order of 0.2–1.5% per month gross on diversified portfolios, mostly decayed by half or more since publication and largely absorbed by costs at scale. Triple-digit annual returns with a 25–30% win rate are not something the literature's premia can produce; they would have to come from concentration in right-tail outcomes, leverage, regime timing and discretionary selection — or luck in a favourable regime.

### Cited Findings (quantitative anchors used below)
- Momentum, long leg: value-weighted winner decile minus market 0.56%/mo (1927–2024) but 0.21%/mo, t=1.0, since 2000; UMD −28% cumulative Jan 2009–Jul 2026. **[D]** — [10 Portfolios Prior 12-2](https://github.com/WhitesPhD/momentum-crashes-replication/blob/main/CodeBase/Data/FF_mom_monthly.CSV); [F-F Momentum Factor](https://github.com/sywon03-prog/Momentum_Project/blob/main/data/raw/F-F_Momentum_Factor.csv)
- Momentum, small caps: 0.58%/mo (t=2.4) in 2010–Jun 2026. **[D]** — [Ken French daily momentum files](https://github.com/z26qin/momentum-tail-risk-monitor/tree/main/data)
- Post-publication L-S returns (gross, to 2023): JT momentum 0.80%/mo (t=1.7); 52-week high 0.18 (t=0.6); trend factor 0.20 (t=0.5); announcement return 0.85 (t=7.5); SUE 0.39 (t=4.7); short-term reversal 1.63 (t=4.1); MAX 0.34 (t=0.6); short interest 0.93 (t=3.7). **[D]** — [OSAP PredictorSummary2024.xlsx](https://github.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/blob/master/Signals/pyCode/StataComparison/PredictorSummary2024.xlsx)
- Regime dependence: UMD +0.76%/mo after up markets, −0.44%/mo after down markets; −4.05%/mo in bear-market rebound months. **[D]** — [F-F Momentum Factor](https://github.com/sywon03-prog/Momentum_Project/blob/main/data/raw/F-F_Momentum_Factor.csv)
- Recent regime: winner decile +47.8% in 2020, −5.1% in 2021, −13.4% in 2022; winner-decile drawdown −43% from Feb 2021 to Jul 2022; equal-weighted *loser* decile +104.5% in 2020. **[D]** — [10 Portfolios Prior 12-2](https://github.com/WhitesPhD/momentum-crashes-replication/blob/main/CodeBase/Data/FF_mom_monthly.CSV); [Ken French daily momentum files](https://github.com/z26qin/momentum-tail-risk-monitor/tree/main/data)
- Drift is gone in non-microcaps since 2006. **[S]** — [Martineau 2022](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0122)
- Shorting premium 1.43%/mo gross, 0.91% net; anomalies absent in the 80% of low-fee stocks. **[S]** — [NBER w20282](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20282)
- Skewness: 4% of stocks create all net wealth. **[S]** — [ASU summary of Bessembinder](https://wpcarey.asu.edu/department-finance/faculty-research/do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)
- Stops on momentum: worst month −49.8% → −11.3%, mean 1.01 → 1.73%/mo, gross. **[S]** — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/)
- Intraday breakout benchmark: Sharpe 2.81, 41.6% a year, 2016–2023, working paper. **[S]** — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/chf/rpseri/rp2498.html)
- Trader base rates: 97% of persistent Brazilian day traders lost money. **[S]** — [SSRN 3423101](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101)

### Inferences

#### Setup 1 — Momentum breakouts in leading stocks
- **(i) Anomalies plausibly harvested:** cross-sectional price momentum (long leg), industry/theme momentum, nearness to the 52-week high, short-term continuation in high-turnover stocks, and the stronger momentum found in young, small, high-uncertainty firms.
- **(ii) Realistic effect sizes:** gross, the winner decile has beaten the market by about 6–7% a year over a century but only 2.5–3.5% a year since 2000 (not significant); in equal-weighted data the post-1993 L-S spread is about 0.8%/mo, of which the winner side contributes only 0.05–0.15%/mo over the median stock. Net of costs at institutional scale, estimates run from about half the gross spread (Novy-Marx & Velikov, in-sample) to about zero (Chen & Velikov; Patton & Weller). The breakout *event* and chart pattern add nothing that has been tested.
- **(iii) Known failure regimes:** after down markets and especially in rebounds out of bear markets (2009; Apr and Nov 2020; Jan 2023); sharp leadership rotations (Jul–Sep 2026: −18% on the daily factor); sideways, high-volatility tapes; the winner decile's own drawdowns of 43% (2021–22) and 30% (early 2025).
- **(iv) Beyond the literature:** everything that turns a 3–10% a year tilt into three digits — picking the handful of names that go up several-fold, holding them in large, margined positions, adding to winners, and being largely out of the market in hostile regimes. The 2020 environment (winner decile +48%, equal-weighted losers +104%) rewarded exactly this exposure; 2021–22 punished it.

#### Setup 2 — Episodic pivots
- **(i) Anomalies plausibly harvested:** post-earnings-announcement drift in its announcement-return form, analyst-revision momentum, drift after information-backed price jumps, the high-volume/attention effect, and under-reaction amplified by inattention and by the disposition effect.
- **(ii) Realistic effect sizes:** classic estimates are about 4% per quarter L-S (Bernard & Thomas) and 7.5–12.5% a year abnormal for announcement-return sorts (Brandt et al.); post-publication the top announcement-return quintile has beaten the middle by about 5% a year, equal-weighted and gross. In liquid non-microcaps the drift is reported as zero since 2006, and in illiquid names costs take 70–100%.
- **(iii) Known failure regimes:** large, heavily covered stocks where the gap already contains the news; gaps without fundamental information (reversal branch); crowded, optioned names; and weak tapes, where long-side momentum of every kind fails.
- **(iv) Beyond the literature:** the claim that one can identify, on the morning of the gap, the few names that will run 50–200% over the following weeks. The academic drift is an average of a few percent; the multi-bagger outcome is the right tail of that distribution, and selecting it is discretionary. This is nonetheless the setup with the firmest academic footing.

#### Setup 3 — Parabolic shorts
- **(i) Anomalies plausibly harvested:** short-term reversal, the MAX/lottery effect, attention-driven overpricing, reversal of no-information price shocks, and overpricing sustained by short-sale constraints.
- **(ii) Realistic effect sizes:** extreme recent winners and lottery stocks underperform the median by roughly 0.5–1%/mo gross (prior-month winner decile 0.41 vs ≈1.1%/mo; highest-MAX decile 0.30 vs ≈1.1%/mo; −4.7% over 20 days for attention-herding stocks). Net of borrow fees the shorting premium is about a third smaller on average and close to zero in the highest-fee names, which are the ones that go parabolic.
- **(iii) Known failure regimes:** squeezes and crowded shorts (January 2021); manias in which run-ups extend far beyond the first signal (1999–2000, 2020–21); run-ups backed by real news; fee spikes, recalls and unavailable borrow.
- **(iv) Beyond the literature:** timing. The evidence says extreme run-ups usually end badly, not when; nothing tests an intraday "first crack" entry. The trade's loss distribution has the fat tail on the wrong side, so tight risk limits and small size are doing most of the work in any successful record.

#### What the claimed results would require
- A 25–30% win rate is consistent with the documented skewness of stock returns and with practitioner trend-following results; it is not evidence for or against skill.
- The arithmetic is demanding: at a 25–30% win rate, average winners of 3–5R are needed for an expectancy of 0.2–0.5R per trade, and roughly 70–140R of net profit per year is needed to double an account at 0.5–1% risk per trade. No systematic strategy in the peer-reviewed literature shows that expectancy net of costs; the closest (an intraday opening-range strategy, Sharpe 2.8, about 42% a year) is an eight-year working-paper backtest.
- Factor premia of 0.2–1%/mo cannot be levered into triple digits without taking the factor's crash risk many times over (single months of −12% to −34% on the unlevered L-S factor since 2009; −43% on the unlevered winner decile in 2021–22). So triple-digit years imply some combination of high concentration, margin, selection skill within the anomaly, and a favourable regime.
- Base rates say such outcomes are rare but not impossible: under 1% of studied short-horizon traders are persistently profitable, and that tail does show persistence. From outcomes alone, the literature cannot distinguish a member of that tail from a survivor of a large population of concentrated, levered momentum traders who met 2020–21.

### Gaps
- No academic study tests any of the three setups as they are commonly described (entry on the opening-range break, low-of-day stop, partial exits, moving-average trail — my understanding, not sourced in this note). The mapping from each setup to an anomaly is my inference.
- His claimed returns and win rate are taken from the research brief, not verified here.
- Because full texts could not be opened and the search budget ran out, every [M] figure in this file should be checked against the linked paper before it is quoted as exact in the final report. Highest-priority checks: Bernard & Thomas (1989) 4.2%/60 days; Chan–Jegadeesh–Lakonishok six-month spreads; Novy-Marx & Velikov momentum cost figures; Frazzini–Israel–Moskowitz capacity figures; Barber et al. (2014) 61/38 basis points and "<1%"; Greenwood–Shleifer–You crash probabilities; D'Avolio fee distribution.
